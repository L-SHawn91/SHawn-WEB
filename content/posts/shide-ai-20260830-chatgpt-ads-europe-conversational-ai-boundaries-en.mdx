## Introduction: What’s Changing, and What Still Isn’t Confirmed

OpenAI is expanding ChatGPT Ads to 31 European countries. OpenAI says ads will not shape ChatGPT’s answers or hand conversation content to advertisers. Those statements come from OpenAI, and no independent audit accompanies them.

On August 18, 2026, OpenAI announced that ChatGPT Ads would reach 31 European countries the following week, including Germany, France, Spain, Italy, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, and Austria. It’s the largest expansion since the ad test began in the United States back in February.

This piece separates what OpenAI’s announcements actually establish from what they don’t. Working from four official OpenAI posts, it walks through four points where a boundary sits between what’s been stated and what remains unverified: the scope of the rollout, the independence of answers from ads, how conversation data is handled, and how ad performance gets measured.

---

## What the 31-Country Expansion Does and Doesn’t Confirm

OpenAI’s own timeline shows how this built up. The ad test started in the U.S. on February 9, 2026, for logged-in adults. On March 26, OpenAI announced plans to expand into Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. On May 7, it announced plans for the U.K., Mexico, Brazil, Japan, and South Korea. On August 11, it confirmed that ChatGPT Ads had actually launched in those five markets.

Over roughly six months, OpenAI expanded into eight markets beyond the U.S. The newly announced European expansion covers 31 countries, more than those eight markets combined, and OpenAI calls it its biggest expansion to date.

One thing worth flagging: the August 18 announcement says the rollout begins “next week.” It is not a confirmation that all 31 countries are live, on the same day, under identical conditions. The list of countries in the announcement is also introduced with the word “including,” meaning the full list of 31 has not been published.

European countries also differ from one another in privacy law and advertising regulation. OpenAI’s announcement communicates scope and timing. It says nothing about individual regulators having reviewed or approved the product market by market. One launch post is not enough to conclude that an identical, fully settled service is already running across the whole region.

OpenAI also describes the moments it is trying to reach: planning a trip, comparing software for a company, choosing furniture for a home, or beginning to learn a new concept or hobby. In those moments, people describe their goals and constraints in full sentences rather than entering a few search terms. OpenAI presents that as an opportunity for advertisers to participate across exploration, comparison, and a possible purchase journey.

## Who Sees Ads and Who Doesn’t

ChatGPT Ads doesn’t appear the same way for every user. OpenAI says ads are shown only to people on the Free plan and the low-cost ChatGPT Go plan. Plus, Pro, and Enterprise subscribers use ChatGPT without ads.

This split has held since the February test began. At that point OpenAI said Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise, and Education tiers would not carry ads, and the August European announcement restates the same rule for Plus, Pro, and Enterprise.

The free tier has another choice. OpenAI has said Free-tier users who do not want ads can opt out in exchange for fewer daily free messages. Avoiding ads therefore means either upgrading to a paid plan or accepting a lower message limit on the free one.

OpenAI frames this structure as a funding mechanism: ad revenue supports free and low-cost access. Ads, in OpenAI’s account, are part of what keeps that lower-cost tier running.

OpenAI places that funding rationale inside a broader access argument: whether people can use an AI assistant for learning, work, and everyday decisions may shape whether AI expands opportunity or reinforces existing gaps. That is OpenAI’s stated business rationale, not independent evidence that access has actually broadened by a measurable amount.

---

## OpenAI’s Stated Commitment That Ads Don’t Shape Answers

OpenAI has published five ad principles: mission alignment, answer independence, conversation privacy, choice and control, and long-term value. Of these, answer independence is the one that matters most the moment advertising enters a conversational product.

OpenAI states that ads do not influence the answers ChatGPT gives. Answers, it says, are optimized for what’s most helpful to the user, and ads are always shown separately and clearly labeled as sponsored. Screenshots in OpenAI’s own materials show a sponsored card appearing below ChatGPT’s answer, in its own visually distinct block.

Answer independence is a design principle OpenAI has committed to publicly. It is not the result of an outside auditor examining ChatGPT’s actual answers and confirming that recommendations do not shift when an advertiser is involved. OpenAI’s March 26 claim that it saw “no impact on consumer trust metrics” is also based on OpenAI’s internal measurement. No public methodology or full dataset accompanies that statement.

A published principle is not the same as evidence that the principle holds across every conversation, country, and advertiser relationship. OpenAI has made the commitment publicly. These sources do not show that it has passed an independent audit.

Two of the other principles connect to answer independence as well. Mission alignment says the ad business should remain compatible with expanding access to AI, while long-term value says the product should not optimize for time spent in ChatGPT at the expense of user trust and experience. Both are directions OpenAI has set out; neither is demonstrated here through public revenue, engagement, or external audit data.

## What Ad Selection Actually Uses, and What Advertisers Actually Get

OpenAI has also described how it decides which ad to show. According to its testing-phase disclosures, ad matching draws on the topic of the current conversation, a user’s past chats, and past interactions with ads. If someone is researching recipes, for instance, they might see an ad for a meal kit or grocery delivery service.

When multiple advertisers could plausibly be relevant, OpenAI says it selects and shows the one it judges most relevant first. That selection happens inside OpenAI’s own ad system. Agency and technology partners help with budgeting and creative, but they don’t decide which ad gets shown to whom.

The inputs to ad selection differ from what advertisers receive afterward. OpenAI’s system draws on a broader set of signals to choose an ad, while advertisers receive aggregate metrics such as impressions and clicks. They do not receive the conversation that produced those events or the user’s exact question.

OpenAI’s published examples make the distinction concrete. A conversation about preparing a potluck is paired with a grocery advertiser’s enchilada meal-kit card, while a conversation about visiting Santa Fe is paired with a lodging card. In each example, the ad card carries the product’s statement that ads do not affect answers and that conversations remain private.

## Why Conversations Don’t Reach Advertisers

Conversation privacy is one of the five principles OpenAI lists explicitly. Advertisers, OpenAI says, do not get access to a user’s chats, chat history, memory, or personal details. Across multiple posts, OpenAI says advertisers see aggregate performance data such as view and click counts.

OpenAI also states that it does not sell user data to advertisers. Matching a conversation’s topic to a relevant ad happens inside OpenAI’s own systems; the underlying conversation content itself is not passed to the advertiser as part of that process.

Advertisers do not receive individual conversations or personally identifying details, but conversation data still plays a role in choosing which ad appears. Current and past conversations feed ad matching. OpenAI says it uses that data internally for selection without handing the same data to the advertiser.

OpenAI also says that, as the ad program expands, privacy and user safety will remain central to its design and rollout. It describes safeguards intended to prevent highly narrow targeting of users. At this stage, that is a stated direction; the public materials do not show how the safeguard operates across every campaign.

## User Controls, and Where Ads Aren’t Allowed to Appear

OpenAI describes several controls users have over the ad experience. Ads can be dismissed on the spot. Users can check why a particular ad appeared, leave feedback, delete their ad data with a single action, and toggle personalization on or off at any time.

Separately from these controls, OpenAI also designed zones where ads don’t run at all. During the test, accounts where a user self-identified as under 18, or where OpenAI’s systems predicted the user was under 18, did not see ads. Ads were also not made eligible to appear near sensitive or regulated topics such as health, mental health, or politics.

This boundary traces back to the design principles OpenAI published when the test plan was first announced in January. Later updates repeat that OpenAI intends to expand these safeguards “responsibly” as they mature, but the August European announcement does not reconfirm the boundary country by country. The principle belongs to the ad program’s original design; these sources do not show a fresh market-by-market verification across Europe.

OpenAI also mentions advertiser-review processes intended to screen out fraudulent ads and says user safety should take priority over program expansion. Its ad-history screen is described as letting people review the ads they saw that day by time and brand, then clear that history. Those are product commitments described by OpenAI, not public evidence that every screening decision or control operates identically in every market.

## How Advertisers Actually Buy ChatGPT Ads

Alongside the European expansion, OpenAI has also widened how advertising gets purchased. Initially, only the OpenAI Ads Solutions team and a small group of agencies handled campaigns. Now, agency partners including Dentsu, Omnicom, Publicis, and WPP work alongside technology partners including Adobe, Criteo, Kargo, Pacvue, and StackAdapt.

OpenAI is also rolling out a beta self-serve tool, Ads Manager, to more advertisers over time. Businesses can register an account, add payment details, set budgets and bids, upload creative, and track campaign performance directly. OpenAI intends the path to serve small startups as well as large brands without requiring an agency.

The buying mechanics have expanded too. Advertisers originally could buy only on a CPM basis, or cost per thousand impressions. OpenAI has since added CPC bidding, or cost per click, along with conversion optimization, geo-targeting, and custom audience tools. OpenAI says tens of thousands of marketers have now run ads on ChatGPT through these combined channels.

OpenAI explains the move from CPM to CPC by saying that many ChatGPT conversations are active and decision-oriented: users compare categories, consider options, and think about what to do next. In OpenAI’s account, a click in that setting can signal that an ad was relevant and helped a user proceed. Under CPC, advertisers are charged only when a click occurs.

Geo-targeting and custom-audience tools are presented as ways for advertisers to allocate a budget more precisely toward relevant customer groups. They may improve buying-side efficiency, but they do not establish that an ad helped a particular user or that the advertised option was the best one.

## What Clicks and Conversions Don’t Prove

Measurement tools have expanded in parallel. With the OpenAI Pixel and Conversions API, advertisers can track actions after someone engages with an ad, including a purchase or sign-up. Third-party measurement integrations are also part of this expanded toolkit.

A click or reported conversion is a measurement event. It is not evidence that ChatGPT’s answer caused a purchase, and it does not show that the advertised product was the best available option. Clicks and conversions record what a user did. They do not explain why or establish the quality of the recommendation.

A concrete case makes this easier to see. Say a Free-plan user in Vienna, Austria, asks ChatGPT about a weekend trip to Lisbon. ChatGPT answers with weather and things to do, and below that answer, a separately labeled sponsored card for accommodations appears. The user can check why that card showed up, dismiss it, or click through to learn more. If they click and end up booking, the advertiser receives an aggregate record showing one conversion.

What that record can’t tell you is whether ChatGPT’s travel answer is what drove the booking, or whether the user was already looking at Lisbon accommodations before asking. It also can’t tell you whether that particular listing was actually the best option among the alternatives. A conversion count confirms that something happened. It doesn’t explain what caused it.

## Conclusion: Answering the Opening Question, and What Remains Unknown

Advertising changes a conversational AI product in specific, testable ways. Within what OpenAI has disclosed, Free and Go users in 31 European countries will see ads displayed separately from ChatGPT’s answers, while paid-plan users continue using ChatGPT without ads. The current conversation and past chat history feed ad selection, but OpenAI says advertisers receive aggregated performance data rather than individual conversations. Users can dismiss ads, see why they were shown, and delete their ad data.

Real gaps remain. Answer independence, the claim that ads do not shape what ChatGPT tells a user, is OpenAI’s stated commitment rather than a finding from an outside auditor. OpenAI’s March claim of “no impact on trust metrics” came without a public methodology or full dataset. The announcement alone does not confirm that all 31 European countries are live at the same time and on the same terms. Clicks and conversions describe what users did, not why they did it or whether the underlying recommendation was good.

The useful checks are rollout scope, answer independence, conversation-data handling, and the limits of performance metrics. Each has a gap between OpenAI’s public statements and independent confirmation. Readers should keep that gap visible when judging advertising inside a conversational AI product.
