## The dispute starts with a KRW 1.4 billion versus KRW 900 million deduction

The core issue is the comprehensive real-estate-tax basic deduction for a one-home owner who does not live in that home. In the [Ministry of Finance and Economy’s official 2026 tax-reform explanation](https://mofe.go.kr/nw/mosfnw/mvpnesDetail.do?searchNttId1=MOSF_000000000078979&menuNo=4040300), the deduction for an owner-occupied one-home household rises from KRW 1.2 billion to KRW 1.4 billion, while the proposed deduction for a non-occupying one-home owner falls from KRW 1.2 billion to KRW 900 million.

That creates a KRW 500 million gap between two one-home owners based on occupancy. The government describes the proposal as a move toward residence-centered taxation. The governing party has argued that work, education, caregiving and medical treatment can force genuine owners to live elsewhere and that the rule may also affect rental supply.

The basic deduction is the amount subtracted from a home’s assessed value before the comprehensive real-estate tax is calculated, so a larger deduction shrinks the amount actually subject to tax. Changing the deduction threshold means that two homes with the same assessed value can end up on opposite sides of the taxable line depending only on whether the owner lives there.

The one-home category discussed here is measured at the household level and is separate from the deduction and rate structure that applies to owners of multiple homes. This debate is therefore not about tightening rules on multi-home ownership; it is about how a single-home household is treated differently depending on occupancy.

A headline saying the burden will be eased does not mean the tax law has already changed. The government proposal, party-government negotiations and parliamentary review remain separate stages, and the number that will appear in the final statute is not yet settled.

That is why the same story can be summarized two ways: “relief for one-home owners” and “a tougher rule for non-occupying one-home owners.” Neither description is wrong. They refer to two different groups — owner-occupiers and non-occupying owners — inside the same proposal.

## What the government proposal contains

The questions identified in the [23 August MBC report](https://imnews.imbc.com/replay/2026/nwdesk/article/6846698_37004.html) and the [25 August SBS Biz seed report](https://v.daum.net/v/20260825133614795) extend beyond one deduction. The proposal lowers the non-occupying one-home deduction to KRW 900 million, raises the comprehensive real-estate-tax burden cap from 150% to 200%, and moves the fair-market-value ratio to 70%.

The fair-market-value ratio determines how much of a home’s assessed value actually enters the tax base, and the burden cap sets the maximum multiple by which this year’s bill can exceed last year’s. When both figures move at the same time, the size of a tax increase can grow independently of whatever happens to the basic deduction, so reading the deduction number alone can miss how the cap and the ratio interact.

For capital-gains tax, the proposal shifts long-term deductions from holding-period treatment toward residence-period treatment. The ministry says residence can still receive a maximum ten-year, 80% deduction, while non-residence periods receive less favorable treatment, meaning the deduction applied to a sale’s profit shrinks for years spent owning but not living in the home.

The comprehensive real-estate tax is billed annually simply for holding a property, while capital-gains tax applies only at the point of sale. The proposal moves both taxes in the same direction — toward occupancy as the organizing principle — which is why the two changes read as one connected design rather than two unrelated adjustments.

This adjustment is one item inside the larger 2026 tax-reform package. The ministry’s official explanation covers other tax categories beyond the comprehensive real-estate tax and capital-gains tax, but this article covers only the locked claims related to non-occupying one-home taxation.

## Unavoidable non-occupancy: the conditions behind the exception

The design also includes exceptions for unavoidable non-occupancy, such as education, work assignment, medical treatment or convalescence. In the ministry’s public explanation, those periods could count as residence when the owner had lived there for at least one year before moving, with recognition capped at three years.

Splitting the condition into its parts shows two separate requirements. First, the owner must have actually lived in the home for at least one year before the non-occupancy period began. Second, the non-occupancy period that can be recognized afterward is capped at a maximum of three years.

This is not a structure that treats every reason for non-occupancy the same way; an owner has to clear both the prior-residence threshold and the time cap before the exception applies. These are exactly the two conditions the governing party wants to loosen.

For a household that moved elsewhere for schooling or a work posting and plans to return to the original home, whether this exception applies could produce a real difference in tax burden. What documentation or procedure would be used to prove an unavoidable reason, however, is not yet spelled out in the public record.

## What the governing party asked to reconsider

The party-government meeting reached common ground on expanding the circumstances treated as unavoidable non-occupancy. Some governing-party lawmakers also asked that the one-home deduction be set at KRW 1.4 billion regardless of occupancy.

Other requests included keeping the burden cap at the current 150% rather than raising it to 200%, and softening the planned reduction in capital-gains deductions for non-residence periods. These are negotiating positions, not enacted rules.

Lined up together, the three requests point in one direction: unify the deduction at KRW 1.4 billion regardless of occupancy, hold off on raising the burden cap, and reduce the capital-gains penalty for non-residence periods — all more favorable to non-occupying one-home owners than the government’s original proposal.

If these requests were adopted as written, the deduction gap between occupied and non-occupying one-home owners that the original proposal emphasized would narrow considerably. The reconsideration is ultimately a disagreement between the government and the governing party over how strictly to enforce residence-centered taxation.

A party-government meeting is a political coordination process, not a legally binding one. For any request raised there to become statutory language, it still has to pass through cabinet submission, formal introduction to the National Assembly, and committee review.

## What the 30 August parliamentary-stage update shows

A [30 August follow-up report](https://www.mt.co.kr/politics/2026/08/30/2026083009475491114) said the government was still expected to advance its original bill while the governing party considered changes during parliamentary review. The more accurate description is therefore that the decision has moved toward the National Assembly, not that the government proposal has already been rewritten.

This update matters because of where it sits procedurally. Submitting the bill to the cabinet is the step that finalizes the government’s version before it moves to the National Assembly — it is not a sign that the governing party’s reconsideration requests have already been incorporated. Those requests remain something to be handled, if at all, as amendments during parliamentary review.

At this point, the government’s original numbers and the governing party’s requested numbers should not be read as belonging to the same finished bill. One is the version that passed cabinet review; the other is a proposed amendment still under discussion in the legislature. Parliamentary review could settle on either figure, or land on a compromise number between them.

## The rental-market argument remains a hypothesis

Supporters of relief argue that a lower holding burden may reduce the incentive for owners to move into their rental properties, helping preserve rental supply. Critics argue that broad relief could weaken the principle of residence-centered taxation for high-value housing.

The current public record cannot quantify how a tax revision would change rental supply or deposits. Move-in volume, financing rates, credit rules, local supply and contract-renewal timing all move alongside tax policy.

It is therefore too early to say that relief would solve the rental shortage or necessarily raise home prices. What is confirmed is narrower: concern about rental supply has entered the political negotiation over the tax design.

This is a dispute over whether tax design can influence the rental market, not a claim that one tax change alone determines the direction of rents or deposits. The public record confirms only that rental-supply concern has been placed on the negotiating table alongside the tax changes.

Judging the actual effect of a tax revision on the rental market requires real leasing-market data collected after the change takes effect. No such after-the-fact data exists yet, so claims about rental-supply effects should be read as forecasts or policy expectations rather than confirmed outcomes.

Whether an owner actually moves into a property or keeps renting it out depends on factors beyond tax burden, including children’s schooling, workplace location and broader asset-allocation choices. A tax change alone should not be read as settling the direction of the rental market.

## Comparing scenarios: structure only, no personal calculation

Comparing three scenarios by structure alone makes it easier to see where the argument actually sits. None of this calculates an individual’s tax bill; it only tracks which direction the basic deduction and the burden cap move under each scenario.

First, the occupied one-home scenario under the government proposal raises the basic deduction from KRW 1.2 billion to KRW 1.4 billion. Second, the non-occupying one-home scenario under the government proposal lowers the basic deduction from KRW 1.2 billion to KRW 900 million while raising the burden cap from 150% to 200%.

Third, the non-occupying one-home scenario under the governing party’s request sets the deduction at KRW 1.4 billion regardless of occupancy and keeps the burden cap at the current 150%. All three scenarios differ only in the baseline figures that feed a later calculation, not in an actual tax bill.

Placed side by side, the three scenarios reduce the argument to two axes: how far the basic deduction should fall for non-occupancy alone, and how high a cap should let a bill grow relative to the prior year.

Which scenario ends up in the final statute depends on the outcome of parliamentary review, and treating the three scenarios as interchangeable to estimate a personal tax bill would be premature. The scenarios differ only in the starting baselines for a calculation; what a specific household actually owes can only be computed once the assessed value and individual eligibility are settled.

## Key terms explained

A short glossary helps when reading coverage of this proposal, since the same terms recur across reports on the government proposal, the party-government requests and the parliamentary review.

- Basic deduction : the amount subtracted from a home’s assessed value before the comprehensive real-estate-tax base is calculated.

- Fair-market-value ratio : the share of assessed value that actually enters the taxable base.

- Burden cap : the maximum multiple by which this year’s tax bill can exceed last year’s.

- Long-term deduction for capital gains : the deduction subtracted from a sale’s profit, sized according to holding and residence periods.

- Unavoidable non-occupancy exception : the rule that lets certain periods away from the home — for schooling, work or medical treatment — count as residence under set conditions.

Keeping these five terms distinct makes it much easier to follow which calculation element a given figure in the government proposal, the party-government requests, or the parliamentary coverage is actually referring to. The basic deduction and the burden cap in particular sit at different stages of the calculation, so a report that mentions both should not be read as describing a single number.

## Common misunderstandings, corrected

One frequent misunderstanding is that the KRW 1.4 billion deduction applies to every one-home owner. In the government proposal, KRW 1.4 billion is the figure for owner-occupiers, while the proposed figure for non-occupying one-home owners is KRW 900 million. Collapsing the two into a single number produces the opposite impression of the actual policy direction.

A second misunderstanding is that non-occupying one-home owners will automatically pay more comprehensive real-estate tax. The actual bill depends on assessed value, the fair-market-value ratio, tax rates and the burden cap acting together, so the basic deduction alone cannot determine whether a final bill rises or falls.

A third misunderstanding treats the government proposal and the governing party’s requests as one already-agreed reform. As of 30 August, the public record shows the government submitting its original bill to the cabinet while the governing party’s requests remain matters to be addressed during parliamentary review.

Finally, some coverage describes the effect of a smaller non-occupancy deduction on the rental market as an already-confirmed fact. As explained above, that remains a hypothesis under debate, and it should not be treated as a settled causal relationship until real data after implementation can test it.

Most of these misunderstandings come from merging numbers or subjects that belong to different stages: occupancy status, the government proposal versus the governing party’s requests, and enacted law versus review still in progress. Keeping those categories separate resolves most of the confusion on its own.

## What owners should check now

Owners should not use either KRW 900 million or KRW 1.4 billion as a final personal deduction before legislation is enacted. They need to distinguish the government bill from parliamentary amendments and check the assessment date, assessed value, occupancy exceptions and ownership structure.

If a period of non-occupancy stems from schooling, a work assignment or medical treatment, the order of operations is to first confirm whether the prior residence period reached at least one year and whether the non-occupancy period stays within the three-year cap. Failing either condition can make the exception difficult to claim.

Rules that depend on household structure, such as joint ownership or rental registration, can change how the proposal applies, so working through a case-by-case check with the tax authority or a tax professional is worth starting now rather than waiting until parliamentary review ends. Household composition or residence status right before the assessment date can carry directly into whether a deduction applies.

If the reason for non-occupancy falls outside education, work or medical treatment, it is unlikely to qualify under the exception the government has proposed so far. Anyone whose situation is ambiguous is better off waiting for the enforcement decree’s detailed rules rather than assuming eligibility before the law is promulgated.

## What tenants should check now

Tenants should not assume that an existing agreement changes because of a tax headline. Any effect on supply or deposits can vary by location and contract date, so renewal timing, guarantees, financing conditions and actual listings remain separate checks.

That a lower holding burden for landlords is under discussion does not mean a tenant’s deposit or monthly rent will drop right away. Tax policy and rent levels respond to different market forces, so the two should not be treated as a single cause-and-effect relationship. Even if a landlord’s tax burden falls, whether that saving flows into lower rent, other spending, or a landlord’s broader asset plans is an individual landlord’s choice.

A tenant approaching a lease renewal should check the renewal date and local rental-listing trends first, rather than waiting for the tax reform to be finalized, and should review whether a lease-deposit guarantee is in place and on what terms. The longer the tax debate runs, the more the eventual effective date of any new law may drift from an individual tenant’s renewal date.

## Documents to watch next

The surest way to avoid reading the government proposal, the party-government requests and the parliamentary-review reporting as one finished law is to track the documents still to come. The cabinet-approved bill shows the official text of what the government will submit to the National Assembly.

The bill submitted to the National Assembly and the review materials from the relevant standing committee are where it becomes clear whether the governing party’s requested KRW 1.4 billion unified deduction or the request to keep the burden cap unchanged actually made it in. The statutory text promulgated after a plenary vote, along with the enforcement decree and enforcement rules, will finally settle the detailed conditions for the exception.

Additional press releases or explanatory materials from the Ministry of Finance and Economy are also worth watching. The specific list of qualifying unavoidable non-occupancy reasons and the procedure for claiming them may be defined in more detail at the enforcement-decree stage.

Following news coverage alone can make the government proposal, the governing party’s requests and any parliamentary amendments look blended together. Using the document list above as a reference instead makes it possible to check, independently, how far the process has advanced and which figures remain unsettled.

## Conclusion: follow the decision stage, not one headline number

What is clear is not that the government proposal has become law, but that the principle and exceptions for taxing non-occupying one-home owners are being renegotiated. The government proposal strengthens residence-centered taxation, while the governing party wants more room for unavoidable non-occupancy and possible rental-supply effects.

The next documents to watch are the cabinet-approved bill, the bill submitted to the National Assembly, committee amendments and the final promulgated text. Until then, KRW 1.4 billion, KRW 1.2 billion and KRW 900 million belong to different policy options and should not be collapsed into one settled rule.

Owners and tenants need the same posture here. Rather than anchoring a conclusion to one number, the more accurate approach at this stage is to first identify whether that number belongs to the government proposal, the party-government request, or an amendment still under parliamentary review. Every figure in this article should be treated as provisional until the statutory text is actually promulgated.

The terms, stages, scenarios and owner-and-tenant checklist covered here form a framework that will keep applying to the cabinet-approved bill and parliamentary coverage still to come. The most reliable habit going forward is checking, for each new report, which stage and which group it is actually describing.
