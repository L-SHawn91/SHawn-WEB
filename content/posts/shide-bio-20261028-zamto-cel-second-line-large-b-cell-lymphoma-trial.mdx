For people whose lymphoma returns after first treatment, a transplant may not be an option. A European trial has now compared a cell therapy designed to recognise CD20 and CD19 with a chemotherapy-based regimen in that setting. The result is a useful piece of evidence, but it still comes from a phase 2 study and needs to be read alongside the treatment harms and the length of follow-up.

## The randomised comparison favoured zamto-cel for event-free survival

**Study design:** Open-label, multicentre, randomised phase 2 trial at 45 centres in 12 European countries.

**Enrolled population:** 160 adults with second-line relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma who were ineligible for transplantation; 82 were assigned to zamto-cel and 78 to R-GemOx.

**Analysis population:** All randomised participants for the primary event-free survival comparison; safety was assessed in participants who received treatment.

**Primary endpoint:** Event-free survival assessed by a masked independent review committee.

 Event-free survival is the time until an event defined by the trial is recorded.

The comparison included all 160 people assigned to a group, rather than only those who completed treatment. Median event-free survival was 6·21 months (95% CI 3·84–13·77) with zamto-cel and 2·53 months (95% CI 1·97–3·35) with R-GemOx. The reported hazard ratio was 0·39 (95% CI 0·27–0·58; p&lt;0·0001), with median follow-up of 17·0 months (IQR 9·0–22·8). These estimates describe time until an event defined by the trial; they are not the same as overall survival or proof that an individual patient will benefit.

Random assignment helps make the two groups more comparable at the start, while review of the primary endpoint by a masked independent committee reduces the chance that knowledge of treatment changes how an event is classified. The study itself was open-label, however, so participants and treating teams knew which treatment was given. That matters when interpreting experience-based outcomes, supportive care, and decisions made during follow-up. The comparison was against R-GemOx, not against every treatment used in this clinical setting, and its findings answer a focused question about one alternative for people who could not undergo transplantation.

## The result addresses a group with limited transplant options

The trial compared zamto-cel, which targets the surface proteins CD20 and CD19, with the named chemoimmunotherapy regimen R-GemOx in adults with second-line relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma who were ineligible for transplantation. Because both groups were part of the same randomised study, the result is a direct comparison within that enrolled population, rather than a comparison assembled across separate studies. It does not establish how zamto-cel compares with every other available treatment or how the result applies to people who were not eligible for this trial.

The difference in median event-free survival is large enough to warrant attention, and the hazard ratio points in the same direction. Still, a group-level median is not a forecast for one person. Some participants had events earlier and others later, and the confidence intervals show uncertainty around each estimate. The reported p value addresses the trial’s statistical comparison, not whether the treatment is right for a particular patient or whether the result will remain unchanged with longer observation.

There is also a practical trade-off. One infusion is not the same as a simple treatment pathway: cell therapy involves preparation and specialist monitoring. The abstract does not compare the full time burden, hospital use, or access barriers between the two strategies, so those practical questions remain open.

For now, the most defensible reading is narrow: in this phase 2 randomised comparison, the zamto-cel group had longer median event-free survival than the R-GemOx group. That is a finding to discuss in the context of an individual diagnosis, prior treatment, fitness, available services, and preferences. It should not be turned into a general claim that CAR T-cell therapy is always better, or that all people with relapsed lymphoma should receive it.

## Serious harms and treatment-related deaths were reported in both groups

Safety is part of the result, not a footnote. Grade 3 or higher treatment-emergent adverse events were reported in 63 (83%) of 76 treated participants receiving zamto-cel and 65 of 76 (86%) receiving R-GemOx. The abstract also reports serious adverse events in 31 (41%) and 50 (66%), respectively, but does not state the denominator in that sentence. We therefore keep the published counts and percentages as given rather than assigning an unstated denominator.

The most frequent grade 3–4 events listed were neutropenia, anaemia, and decreased neutrophil count. Serious events included lymphoma progression, cytokine release syndrome, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome. The abstract gives counts and percentages for those named events, including cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity in the zamto-cel group. These figures describe events observed in this trial; they do not predict precisely what will happen to a future patient.

The authors reported treatment-related deaths in both arms: 2/82 (3%) assigned to zamto-cel, attributed to cytokine release syndrome and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, and 1/78 (1%) assigned to R-GemOx, attributed to lymphoma progression. Those deaths must remain visible when discussing the apparent difference in serious adverse events. The abstract does not provide all-cause death counts or full treatment-related adverse-event rates by arm, so this summary cannot establish a complete safety comparison.

Other limits are just as important. The trial was open-label, took place at specialist centres in Europe, and reports a median follow-up of 17 months. The primary endpoint was reviewed by a masked committee, but longer follow-up may change estimates of durability and later harms. The PubMed abstract identifies the study as ongoing at the time described; it does not establish its current registry status. The phase 2 result is not equivalent to confirmatory evidence from a phase 3 trial, and it does not show that the treatment cures lymphoma.

## Longer follow-up will show how durable the difference is

The next useful information is not simply another headline number. Readers need to know whether the event-free survival difference persists as follow-up grows, how overall survival develops, and whether the safety profile changes with time. The PubMed abstract does not report an overall-survival result, so none should be inferred from the event-free survival figures.

More detail on participant flow would also help. The primary comparison used all randomised participants, while the safety assessment included those who received treatment. The abstract reports 76 treated people per group for grade 3 or higher events, fewer than the numbers assigned to each arm. The reasons and timing behind that difference are not fully described in the abstract. A full report and updated registry record can clarify how many people started treatment, discontinued, or were unavailable for particular assessments.

For clinicians, the study adds a direct comparison in a population where options can be constrained by transplant eligibility. It does not replace an individual assessment of disease course, prior therapy, comorbidities, treatment access, or a patient’s goals. People considering treatment should discuss expected benefit, serious complications, monitoring, and alternatives with their oncology team rather than relying on a single trial summary.

The overall picture is therefore measured: zamto-cel produced a longer median event-free survival than R-GemOx in this randomised phase 2 trial, but follow-up is still limited and serious harms occurred with both treatments. The result strengthens the case for continued evaluation in this group; it does not close the question of long-term benefit, safety, or who is most likely to benefit.
