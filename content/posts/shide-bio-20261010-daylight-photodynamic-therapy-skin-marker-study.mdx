## The signal was narrower than the title may suggest

![Conceptual illustration of biopsy timing](https://shawnbiohub.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/image-5454749d89934dd4-eb9a449a12b3.webp)

_AI-generated conceptual illustration; not study data._

The study compared  daylight photodynamic therapy (DL-PDT), which combines a light-sensitive medicine with light exposure , alone or paired with one of three techniques intended to improve delivery through the skin: microneedling, CO2 laser, or microdermabrasion. The PubMed abstract says 40 patients were randomized, ten to each group. Everyone received two sessions a month apart, using methyl aminolevulinate cream followed by daylight exposure. Researchers sampled skin before treatment and again three months after the second session.

The measurements were tissue markers, not new cancers, recurrence, symptoms, or survival. The team assessed p53 and Ki67, which relate to cell processes, along with collagen and matrix-remodeling markers. The abstract reports that Ki67 staining appeared to extend through a smaller vertical portion of the epidermis mainly in the microneedling and CO2-laser groups. That pattern was not statistically significant. It also reports no significant Ki67 or p53 differences within or between groups.

The one result the abstract identifies as statistically significant was a reduction in TIMP-1 in the overall analysis, with p = 0.041. The abstract does not provide the size of that change or a confidence interval, and it does not identify a prespecified primary endpoint. A p-value alone does not show how large or clinically useful a change was. The result therefore deserves attention as a finding to check, not as proof that one procedure worked better.

**Study design:**  Randomized four-group comparison

**Analysis population:**  Marker-specific denominators are not stated in the abstract

The reported cohort comprised patients with facial field cancerization, and each treatment arm began with ten people. The abstract does not say how many samples contributed to each marker analysis, so ten per assigned arm should not be mistaken for a verified denominator for every result. The reported p-value belongs to the overall TIMP-1 analysis, not to an individual combination arm.

## A tissue-marker change is not a cancer outcome

The random allocation makes the four treatment approaches more suitable for comparison than observations made in unrelated groups, but it does not solve every uncertainty. With ten people assigned to each arm, estimates can be unstable, and the abstract does not describe the allocation process, masking, or whether every randomized participant contributed a biopsy. Those details matter when readers try to judge how much confidence to place in a small between-group difference.

DL-PDT uses a light-sensitive medicine and daylight exposure. In this study, the question was whether adding a skin-delivery technique changed selected markers in biopsy samples. That is a narrower question than whether the treatment prevents skin cancer, removes lesions, or improves how patients feel. The abstract reports no cancer-incidence or patient-reported endpoint. Its measurements can suggest biological changes, but they cannot substitute for clinical outcomes that the study did not report.

The marker pattern was mixed. Some remodeling markers moved upward and others downward, while the abstract singles out only TIMP-1 reduction as statistically significant. A direction of change is not automatically beneficial: the meaning depends on the marker, the size and durability of the change, and whether it corresponds to an outcome patients notice. The abstract does not provide enough information to connect these tissue findings to a reduced chance of future cancer.

This distinction is especially important because the study title includes carcinogenesis. That term describes processes related to cancer development; it does not mean the investigators observed cancer prevention. The paper, as represented in its abstract, examined immunohistochemical markers in skin samples. Readers should keep the result at that level until larger studies connect the markers to clinical outcomes.

## The abstract leaves important clinical questions open

The PubMed abstract does not report adverse-event counts, severity, or comparisons among the four groups. That gap cannot be read as evidence that participants had no adverse events. The accessible record also does not provide marker-specific analysis denominators, effect sizes, confidence intervals, missing-sample handling, or the prespecified primary endpoint. These omissions limit independent assessment of both the strength of the signal and the completeness of safety reporting.

Follow-up tissue collection took place three months after the second treatment session. That window can capture a short-term marker pattern, but it does not establish that the pattern lasts. Nor does a biopsy result tell us whether participants later developed fewer lesions or cancers. The abstract itself describes the findings as exploratory and says they need confirmation in larger randomized studies. That caution fits the small group sizes and the number of markers examined.

A single p-value also needs context. The abstract lists several markers and reports one statistically significant change in the overall analysis. Without the full analysis plan and multiplicity handling, readers cannot tell from the abstract how that result should be weighed against the rest of the marker comparisons. The abstract gives no confidence interval or effect estimate for TIMP-1, so precision and practical magnitude remain unknown.

The publisher’s full text was not accessible for this review, so this account stays within the PubMed record. It does not fill in unreported eligibility criteria, participant flow, detailed safety findings, or regulatory status. These are limits of the available source here, not proof that the complete paper contains no such information. The treatment should not be selected or changed on the basis of this article.

## Clinical outcomes must answer the next question

The next useful evidence would show whether the marker findings can be reproduced, with enough participants in each arm to estimate differences precisely. A report should identify its primary endpoint in advance, provide marker-specific denominators and effect estimates with confidence intervals, and explain how missing biopsies and multiple comparisons were handled. Those details would help distinguish a stable treatment signal from a chance finding among several measurements.

For patients, the more direct question is whether adding a delivery technique changes outcomes that matter in care: lesion burden, recurrence, adverse effects, or the need for further treatment. The abstract does not answer those questions. Longer follow-up would also be needed before a short-term biopsy pattern could support claims about cancer prevention or durable benefit.

The current record supports a modest conclusion. In a small randomized comparison, researchers observed several exploratory tissue-marker patterns, but the Ki67 and p53 results were not significant, and only the overall TIMP-1 reduction crossed the reported significance threshold. The abstract provides no effect size or confidence interval for that result. The new record adds a lead for further study; it does not establish a preferred treatment or a clinical benefit.

Until fuller outcome and safety data are available, DL-PDT with microneedling, laser, or microdermabrasion should be understood here as a set of approaches tested for marker changes, not as proven ways to prevent cancer. Clinicians and patients need evidence from adequately sized trials with transparent outcome reporting before drawing treatment conclusions.
