Within twenty-four hours, Meta [introduced Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full]([https://about.fb.com/news/2026/10/introducing-muse-wonder-models/](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/10/introducing-muse-wonder-models/)) on October 12 and Google [posted the global-availability blog for Google AI 4 Argon](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/Google AI-models/introducing-Google AI-4-argon/) on October 13. Two companies shipping models they are willing to call frontier in the same week is not coincidence; it is the signal that the two definitions of frontier are pulling apart. KO · 한국어 / **EN · English** BILINGUAL PAIR

If a team has one frontier-class model in production today and is trying to decide whether to add another, the two announcements from the second week of October 2026 carry more weight than the news cycle suggests. On October 12 Meta [introduced Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/10/introducing-muse-wonder-models/) as the company’s first frontier-class release. The very next day, October 13, Google posted the global-availability blog for Google AI 4 Argon and shipped it to developers, enterprises, scientists, and Google AI Ultra / Pro subscribers worldwide. Within twenty-four hours two companies shipped models they are willing to call frontier, and they did not describe those models in the same words. This article reads those two first-party posts side by side, anchors every operational claim to a vendor URL, and closes with the small verification batch an operator still has to run before either model goes into a real workflow.

## The same week, two different definitions of frontier

Google’s October 13 post opens by saying Argon is “built to solve your hardest problems” and goes on to define those problems as four axes: complex real-world software engineering, enterprise knowledge work in legal and finance, cybersecurity defense, and science. The same post sets the tone in one sentence: “Argon works longer and harder to solve highly complex scientific, coding, and agentic problems.” The piece that explains what ‘longer’ means comes right after: “an industry-leading 1M unified input-output token limit via the Google AI API.” In operational terms, longer and harder is about a single session holding enough headroom for a multi-step refactor, a long-running legal review, or a multi-file analysis that previously had to be split and stitched back together. Google’s own internal examples show the shape. The Chrome team used Argon to separate core webpage handling from a six-million-line legacy folder and shipped the refactor “in weeks” instead of months. The team behind Google’s Agent Development Kit gave Argon a `.agents/skills` rulebook and a one-paragraph goal and let it work overnight, surfacing optimizations that cut in-memory session prep time by 40 percent and sped up cold imports by 21 percent, both now merged into `google/adk-python`.

Meta’s October 12 post defines frontier differently. The same announcement positions Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full as models “built to deliver state-of-the-art performance on personal and professional agentic tasks — from navigating an insurance claim to making sense of a lease to building a working app.” The piece that explains what ‘personal agentic’ means comes in two named abilities. The first is Active Memory: “the ability to carry what matters to you across conversations and bring back the details that are relevant to the task at hand.” The second is Active Focus: “the ability to keep working toward your goal when a task gets hard, try other approaches, recover from errors, and follow through, asking for clarification when needed and checking in before following through on anything important.” The post wraps this framing with a single phrase: “personal superintelligence for everyone.” In operational terms, Google is shipping a model that does one very long thing very deeply. Meta is shipping a model that remembers you across sessions and does not give up when the task gets hard.

## What the numbers say and what the numbers do not

Google’s blog puts a handful of immediately-comparable numbers on the page. The pricing is the easiest one to anchor: two dollars per million input tokens, ten dollars per million output tokens, with cached input tokens priced at 95 percent off. The benchmark slate is the second. Argon ranks first on the Vals Index (which weights finance, coding, legal, and tax work by U.S. GDP contribution), 77.9 percent on DeepSWE v1.1 for real-world long-horizon software engineering, 51.3 percent on Zapier’s AutomationBench for end-to-end execution across core business functions, and 91.7 percent on LVBench for long-video understanding. The internal speed numbers are the third bucket: 40 percent reduction in-memory session prep and 21 percent faster cold imports in the ADK Python codebase, a 2.85x end-to-end inference speedup on Gemma 4 E2B through vLLM on an A100, taking throughput from 37,900 to over 108,000 tokens per second. The safety numbers are the fourth: 0.7 percent attack success across 15 adaptive attempts on Gray Swan’s Indirect Prompt Injection benchmark, and an automated misalignment monitor that reads the agent’s step-by-step reasoning and actions in real time.

Those four buckets are quotable only inside the boundaries of the same blog. (a) The benchmark rankings are what the same blog measured and reported, not an independent verification across every domain. (b) The ADK Python numbers are tied to one open-source repository and one engineering team; they are not a guarantee that the same shape of speedup will appear in every Python codebase. (c) The 2.85x A100 number is a vLLM-plus-A100 measurement; it does not generalize to other runtimes or hardware. (d) The 0.7 percent Gray Swan result holds for one specific adaptive-attack protocol; it is not a guarantee for every indirect-prompt-injection category. The four caveats travel with the four numbers.

Meta’s blog deliberately publishes fewer immediately-comparable numbers. Instead of benchmarks, the post names two behaviors and describes them in human terms. The launch horizon is the date the post is explicit about: Muse Wonder is available to developers and builders today, October 12, through the Meta Model API and Muse Code. Muse Wonder Full is available today to Muse Code subscribers. The Muse consumer surface — the same Meta product line that already lists Muse Spark — will get Muse Wonder “soon,” with subscribers receiving it first. “Soon” is not given a date in the same post, so reading “soon” as “available to general consumers today” would be outside what the first-party source actually says. Pricing for consumer access, the per-region rollout schedule, and the explicit set of supported languages are also not enumerated in the announcement.

## The Google choice — 1M tokens, knowledge work, and Fairwind cyber defense

The strongest operational promise on the Google side is the 1M unified input-output token limit. The same blog makes the boundary explicit: “We are significantly expanding the model’s output token limit to an industry-leading 1M tokens, up from the previous 64K tokens.” The September 30 phased-rollout post frames that expansion as the difference between a model that finishes a multi-step task in one session and a model that has to be split across sessions and reattached. For an operator, the practical question is whether a representative workload (a long codebase refactor, a long document review, a multi-file analysis) finishes in a single Argon session or has to be split. The first-party post does not benchmark against any specific competing model on that workload, so the comparison has to be measured directly in the environment rather than read off the page.

The same blog makes the cybersecurity policy unusually explicit. Argon ships to Fairwind cyber defenders “without cyber guardrails” so that vulnerability discovery, validation, and patching can use the full frontier capability. Wiz is already using Argon under its Scan for Good initiative, and the post cites two Wiz results: a collaboration with a federal agency that preempted the exposure of sensitive data and government infrastructure, and a chain of healthcare-infrastructure vulnerabilities that the model helped discover and patch before hundreds of thousands of patient records could be exposed through a public file-download bypass. These are examples the vendor cites; they are not evidence that the same shape of outcome will repeat across industries. The “no cyber guardrails” framing applies to the Fairwind partner channel only, so general developer, enterprise, and consumer channels still operate under the standard guardrails. Reading the cyber-defense framing as “any user can use Argon without guardrails” would be outside what the first-party source says.

## The Meta choice — Active Memory and Active Focus as nouns

The Meta announcement puts more weight on how the model behaves with a user over time than on what the model scores on a benchmark. Active Memory is the first named ability. The post defines it as the model’s ability to carry what matters across conversations and bring back the details relevant to the task at hand, which is the operational difference between starting every chat with a blank context and starting every chat with the documents, decisions, and constraints from previous sessions. Active Focus is the second named ability. The post defines it as the model’s ability to keep working when the task is hard, try other approaches, recover from errors, ask for clarification when needed, and check in before following through on anything important. In operational terms, Active Focus is the difference between a one-shot response and a multi-session agent that does not stall when the first attempt fails. The same post ties these two named abilities directly to the concrete workflows that Meta expects Muse Wonder to handle: insurance claim navigation, lease review, personal-finance work, and small-app building.

The same announcement is explicit about three safety commitments. First, the model has been trained to avoid sharing private information without permission. Second, the model declines requests to assist in actually harmful acts. Third, the model preserves the ability for legitimate defenders and security teams to use it for cyber-defense work. Those three commitments are anchored to the [Meta Superintelligence Scaling Framework](https://research.meta.ai/blog/developing-capable-models-responsibly) and the post commits to a fuller safety-evaluation report “soon” without giving a date. The general-consumer price, the per-region launch schedule, and the first-wave supported languages are not given in the same post, so any operator reading needs to verify those points against the Meta Model API documentation and the Muse Code subscription terms directly rather than relying on the announcement alone.

## The decisive difference between the two announcements

Reading both vendor posts side by side surfaces three concrete differences. The first is the weight each post puts on quantification. Google leads with benchmark scores, internal-case percentages, pricing, and safety numbers. Meta leads with two named abilities (Active Memory, Active Focus) and a set of user-scenario phrases that describe what the model does for a person rather than what it scores on a benchmark. The second is who gets access first. Google names developers, enterprises, scientists, researchers, Google AI Ultra and Pro subscribers, and Fairwind cyber defenders in the same announcement, and ships to those channels on the day of the post. Meta names developers, builders, and Muse Code subscribers for the same-day channels, and explicitly defers consumer Muse access with the word “soon.” The third is the line between what is guaranteed and what has to be verified. Google draws that line at “vendor-measured benchmark on the same blog, plus the explicit caveat that other vendors and other environments need their own measurements.” Meta draws that line at “vendor-defined behavior plus the explicit caveat that price, region, consumer channel, and the fuller safety report are not yet given in the same announcement.” The same first-party source is doing different work in each post.

## The small verification batch to run this week

Before either announcement is operationalized, there are five things an operator can check this week. First, whether the 1M unified input-output token limit changes the shape of a representative workload — a long refactor, a long legal review, a multi-file analysis — from “split across sessions and reattached” to “finished in one session.” Pilot one representative workload on Argon and measure the session count before and after. Second, in a multi-model environment, which of the operator’s tasks Argon picks up versus which tasks stay on the existing model. Record the routing pattern in a real workflow, not a synthetic benchmark. Third, what Active Memory and Active Focus look like in a real environment. Run a small pilot on a representative personal workflow — an insurance claim, a lease review, a small app — and check which details from the previous session actually reappear in the next one and whether the model keeps working when the first approach fails. Fourth, the price and channel availability on the Meta side. The Meta announcement does not publish a Meta Model API price list or a Muse Code subscription rate, so confirm both against the API documentation and the subscription terms directly rather than inferring them from the announcement. Fifth, the safety posture. Confirm that Argon’s “no cyber guardrails” channel applies only to Fairwind partners and not to the general developer or enterprise channels, and watch the Meta research blog for the fuller safety-evaluation report that the announcement says will arrive “soon.”

The directional signal from the two posts is clear. Google shipped a frontier model that does one very long thing very deeply; Meta shipped a frontier model that remembers the user and does not give up when the task gets hard. The two definitions of frontier are pulling apart, and that is the news of the week. Whether either shape of frontier fits an operator’s environment is a question the announcement does not answer and the operator has to measure directly.

## A compressed version of the same operational checklist

The five items above can be compressed into a single checklist for the week. (1) Pilot one representative long workload on Argon and measure session-count before and after the 1M unified limit. (2) Record which tasks Argon picks up in a multi-model workflow. (3) Pilot Active Memory and Active Focus on a representative personal workflow and record what actually carries across sessions. (4) Confirm Meta Model API pricing and Muse Code subscription terms directly, since they are not in the announcement. (5) Confirm Fairwind-only scope for Argon’s cyber-guardrail removal, and watch for the Meta fuller safety report. Those five are the gate between “two frontier posts in the news cycle” and “two frontier models in the operator’s environment.”

## What the announcements do not guarantee, stated again

To close the loop, the six areas the two announcements do not guarantee are: (a) Google benchmark rankings are independently measured across every domain. (b) ADK Python, Chrome, and Gemma 4 E2B numbers generalize to every codebase, runtime, or hardware. (c) The Gray Swan 0.7 percent result holds across every indirect-prompt-injection category. (d) Meta Muse Wonder is available to general consumers today. (e) Meta Model API pricing and per-region rollout are identical across markets. (f) The named behaviors — long-horizon depth on Google’s side, Active Memory and Active Focus on Meta’s side — appear automatically in any deployment without operator work. Those six are not first-party-guaranteed; they are the operator’s verification surface.
