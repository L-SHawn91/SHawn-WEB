## The bowel and urinary results did not move together

NRG-GU005 compared two radiation schedules in an international, open-label, randomized phase III trial. The registry lists 698 randomized participants: 345 assigned to moderately hypofractionated intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and 353 to stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT). The trial compared disease control and patient-reported quality of life, including bowel and urinary symptoms.

For the bowel domain, the registry recorded a decline of at least four points by the two-year assessment in 113 of 258 participants with available IMRT data and 101 of 289 with available SBRT data. Those are 43.8% and 34.9%, respectively. The difference favors SBRT in this registry table, but the table reports counts and percentages, not a test of statistical significance.

The urinary-irritation results were similar, though the percentage was slightly higher with SBRT. A decline of at least five points was recorded for 85 of 252 assessed participants assigned to IMRT and 102 of 288 assigned to SBRT, or 33.7% and 35.4%. These percentages are close. They should not be collapsed into a single claim that one schedule protected quality of life across both symptom areas.

 EPIC-26, the Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite-26, measures health-related quality of life.  The questionnaire covers urinary, bowel, sexual, and hormonal domains. Scores range from zero to 100, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. The JAMA abstract calls the trial’s two-year changes minimally clinically important differences; the registry gives the point-change thresholds and counts. The bowel and urinary rows use different thresholds and available participants.

**Study design:** International, open-label randomized phase III trial.

**Enrolled population:** Localized intermediate-risk prostate cancer; 345 assigned to IMRT and 353 to SBRT.

**Analysis population:** At two years, available: bowel 258 IMRT/289 SBRT; urinary irritation 252 IMRT/288 SBRT.

**Primary endpoints:** Registry: two-year EPIC-26 bowel, urinary-irritation and disease-free survival. JAMA: two-year EPIC-26 domains and three-year disease-free survival.

## Treatment schedule is not the only outcome patients weigh

The two approaches use different delivery schedules. Visit burden may matter to patients, but convenience alone does not show which approach is better overall.

The peer-reviewed JAMA report helps put the registry rows in context. Its abstract reports no significant difference in two-year urinary-irritation decline: 35.4% with SBRT versus 33.7% with IMRT, with P=.68. It reports fewer bowel-domain declines with SBRT, 34.9% versus 43.8%, with P=.03. Those reported analyses are consistent in direction with the registry’s available-case counts, but the figures come from the journal report and should be attributed to it rather than treated as a p value supplied by the registry.

The same abstract reports three-year disease-free survival of 92.1% for IMRT and 88.6% for SBRT, with confidence intervals of 88.9%–95.2% and 85.2%–92.1%. The estimate was numerically lower with SBRT. The abstract reports a one-sided log-rank P&lt;.001 but does not give more detail about the test direction in that summary. The authors conclude that SBRT was not superior to IMRT for disease-free survival. This is a counterweight to focusing only on bowel symptoms or treatment visits. A quality-of-life result in one domain does not settle disease-control outcomes.

These findings are useful because they show why a treatment comparison can produce more than one answer. Patients may care about the time spent attending treatment, bowel symptoms, urinary function, sexual function, and the chance of cancer returning. The trial measured several of these outcomes, and they did not all point in the same direction. The registry posting makes detailed tables easier to inspect; the journal article remains necessary for the authors’ statistical interpretation.

## The registry tables need their denominators and safety notes

The two-year symptom counts do not include every randomized participant. The bowel and urinary rows have different denominators, so keep each endpoint paired with the number of participants assessed. Comparing event counts alone, or using the randomized totals as though they were the questionnaire denominators, would give the wrong impression.

The registry also reports two-year disease-free survival estimates of 95.9% for IMRT and 91.4% for SBRT, with 95% confidence intervals of 93.8%–98.1% and 88.4%–94.3%. The estimate is numerically lower with SBRT at both the registry’s two-year timepoint and the paper’s three-year timepoint. These are distinct estimates. The two-year registry estimate shown here is not accompanied by a p value; it should not be used on its own to claim that one treatment improves or worsens survival.

Safety deserves the same care. The registry reports a median follow-up of 3.2 years at the time of analysis. The adverse-event module describes its assessment as non-systematic. It records serious adverse events in 12 of 323 participants in the IMRT group and 3 of 352 in the SBRT group. Because the registry describes this assessment as non-systematic, these counts alone cannot establish which schedule has the lower serious-event risk. The record also lists deaths from any cause during follow-up in 13 of 345 participants at risk in the IMRT arm and 16 of 353 in the SBRT arm. These are not statements that radiation caused those deaths. The registry’s event counts do not establish a treatment-related explanation, and its safety denominator is not identical to every outcome denominator.

That distinction matters especially when a reader sees a number without its context. The registry lists the trial as active but not recruiting; it is not marked complete. The completion date remains an estimate, and registry results are not a substitute for the full trial report or clinical judgment.

## The practical reading is a trade-off, not a winner

For a reader comparing these schedules, the narrow takeaway is that the bowel and urinary questionnaire results differed: the registry recorded fewer two-year bowel declines in the SBRT arm, while urinary-irritation declines were similar. The JAMA analysis adds statistical testing and longer-term disease-free survival results, and its conclusion does not support calling SBRT superior for disease control. Neither source establishes that one schedule is best for every person.

The registry’s new tables are best read alongside the JAMA paper, not instead of it. A useful comparison keeps the specific outcome, assessment time, denominator, confidence interval or p value, and source attached to each number. If any of those pieces changes, the interpretation may change too. Patients should discuss treatment schedules with their oncology team, including the expected number of visits, likely effects on daily life, tumor characteristics, and the limits of the available evidence.

What changed on October 29 was public access to a more detailed registry record. The trial did not suddenly acquire a new result that day, and the posting does not settle every question about long-term control or quality of life. It gives readers another primary-source view of a comparison already reported in the medical literature.
