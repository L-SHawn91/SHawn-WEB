## Bottom line: Gyeonggi’s September CPI held near 3% for the second month, driven by petroleum products and semiconductor-linked durables

Korea’s National Data Agency Gyeonggi Provincial Office released its September 2026 consumer price report on October 10, 2026. According to a news write-up of that release, the consumer price index for Gyeonggi Province stood at 120.64 (2020=100) in September, up 0.3% from the previous month and 3.0% from a year earlier. The provincial figure has stayed in the 3% range for two consecutive months. Petroleum products rose 14.7% and pulled the eight-category industrial products index up 4.4%, marking a second straight 4%-range reading. Semiconductor-linked durables added another 5.2%, with computers at 27.4%. Agricultural and marine products rose only 0.5% on average, although pumpkin, mushrooms, mackerel, and hairtail all moved by double digits inside that group. The living cost index, which tracks the 144 items households buy most often, rose 2.7% from a year earlier. Fresh food prices fell 2.1% over the same comparison. The national September index reached 120.43 with a year-on-year gain of 2.9%.

_A still life of fresh ingredients on a kitchen counter as the article opener for Gyeonggi’s September CPI._

Two facts stand out. First, the energy line is steeper than the headline: gasoline 11.9%, diesel 20.3%, and kerosene 13.8% all sit in double digits. Second, the semiconductor cycle has now reached consumer durables at the household level, with computers up 27.4% and mobile phones up 8.3%. On the table side, headline numbers stay modest but the inside of the food basket is volatile, so the average does not match individual shopping experiences. This article lays those facts beside their sources and marks, throughout, what is directly observed and what is scenario or interpretation.

Below we cover the timing and the units first, then the seven substantive sections, and finally the limits and the watchlist for October. The article keeps definitional and unit details in one place at the top so the body text can stay readable.

- Publication date: October 10, 2026

- Original source: National Data Agency (국가데이터처) Gyeonggi Provincial Office — September 2026 Consumer Price Report

- Reporting outlet: News1 (reporter Yoo Jae-gyu), https://www.news1.kr/local/gyeonggi/6314568

- Data scope: prices observed in September 2026 (the previous monthly release covered August)

- Official press-release channel: https://kostat.go.kr/board.es?mid=a10301010000&bid=213

- Standard time-series page on KOSIS: https://kosis.kr/statHtml/statHtml.do?orgId=101&tblId=DT_1J22001

- Reference channels for cross-checking: Bank of Korea ECOS at https://ecos.bok.or.kr/ and Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy at https://www.motie.go.kr/

Two timing points matter and they should be kept separate. “September data” means the prices collected in September 2026. “Publication date” means October 10, 2026, which is when the figures first became public through Statistics Korea and its provincial offices. So every rate cited in this article is a year-on-year September 2026 versus September 2025 figure unless otherwise marked. The index level 120.64 has 2020 = 100 as its base, which is the convention used in this statistical series.

The data scope splits into Gyeonggi Province and the national average. Both appear in the same release, and the Gyeonggi figures are the focus of the reporting outlet. We treat the National Data Agency as the primary source and News1 as a secondary citation that transcribes the official numbers faithfully.

## 1. Gyeonggi’s September index, two months in the 3% range

Gyeonggi’s September consumer price index reached 120.64 (2020=100), up 0.3% from the previous month and 3.0% from a year earlier. August’s number had already sat in the 3% range, so this is the second consecutive month at 3%. The monthly year-on-year path across 2026 reads as 1.9% January, 2.1% February, 2.2% March, 2.7% April, 3.3% May, 3.3% June, 2.8% July, 3.2% August, and 3.0% September. The series broke through the 3% line in April–May, dipped in July, and then settled in a band around 3% for the rest of the year so far.

The national September index reached 120.43, up 0.3% from the previous month and 2.9% from a year earlier. The national figure is 0.1 percentage points below Gyeonggi, which is a tiny gap rather than a divergent trend. Both series sit on the same side of the 120 line and the same side of the Bank of Korea’s 2% reference, which matters more for the directional reading than the small provincial spread does.

Reading the 3% number requires care. Relative to the 5% peak of 2022–2023, 3% is a clear easing. Relative to the 2% objective, 3% is still meaningfully above. The middle-zone reading is the safer one. No single month fixes the trajectory, and the next two monthly releases will move the average noticeably.

---

## 2. The eight-category industrial block ran at 4.4% — petroleum +14.7% and durables +5.2%

The eight-category industrial products block, which includes petroleum products, processed foods, and durables, ran at 4.4% year on year in September. That matches August and makes it two months in a row in the 4% range. Inside that 4.4%, two sub-blocks moved at different speeds and they pull the average in opposite directions.

![Calm Korean suburban dusk with an unmarked petrol station forecourt and a single parked car.](https://shawnassets.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/mechanism_1536x864-1.webp)

_A muted dusk scene of an unmarked petrol forecourt standing in for the petroleum +14.7% line._

Petroleum products rose 14.7% year on year in September. Within petroleum, gasoline was 11.9%, diesel was 20.3%, and kerosene was 13.8%. Diesel is the steepest of the three and explains much of the energy drag. Kerosene also matters for households that still use it for heating in the colder months, and a 13.8% rise translates quickly into winter bills. The release did not separate the supply, tax, and international price components of these numbers, so this article does not allocate the 14.7% between those channels either.

Durables rose 5.2% year on year. Inside durables, computers posted 27.4% and mobile phones posted 8.3%. Both are direct semiconductor-cycle items and both moved last month as well. The 27.4% reading is striking because it is roughly five times the headline provincial rate. It does not mean the average household sees a 27% rise in their shopping basket, because computers are not bought every month. It does mean the technological component of the durable category is adding visible pressure on the index.

Processed foods were cited as part of the eight-category block, but the release did not isolate a single figure for processed foods. This article does not estimate that figure. The 4.4% average is consistent with strong petroleum and durables and a modest processed-food sub-component, but the exact split between processed foods, textiles, and other industrial lines is not separated in the write-up we rely on.

Agricultural and marine products rose only 0.5% on average, but the inside of that group is much more volatile. The cited item-level rises were pork 5.4%, Korean beef 6.8%, pumpkin 49.4%, mackerel 12.5%, mushrooms 21.1%, and hairtail 16.1%. Pork and beef look calm, but vegetables and fish carry double-digit moves. Because these figures are a single-month year-on-year comparison, weather and catch variation can move them sharply, and a monthly reading alone is not a trend.

## 3. Living cost index and fresh food index, two views of “felt inflation”

The living cost index covers 144 items out of 458 that households buy most often, weighted by purchase frequency and expenditure share. It rose 0.7% from the previous month and 2.7% year on year in September. By construction, it is sometimes called the “felt inflation” index. The 2.7% is below the headline 3.0% for the province, which means the items that households touch most often rose less than the average of all 458 items.

![Quiet Korean apartment corner with a simple open laptop, notebook, plant, and a small paper bag of folded clothing.](https://shawnassets.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/checklist_1536x864-1.webp)

_A quiet Korean apartment corner with a simple open laptop and folded clothing — a still life for the durables +5.2% line._

Inside the living cost index, food rose 1.9% and non-food rose 3.2% year on year. The 1.9% food line is moderate and is consistent with the 0.5% average for agricultural and marine products netted through the weighting. The 3.2% non-food line carries the petroleum and durables signal, which is the same direction as the eight-category industrial block’s 4.4%. Two numbers pull the same way and the alignment is what matters more than either single value.

The fresh food index covers 55 items in fisheries, vegetables, and fruit. It rose 3.4% from the previous month but fell 2.1% from a year earlier. Within fresh food, fisheries rose 7.5%, vegetables rose 2.2%, and fruit fell 10.8%. Fisheries and fruit moved in opposite directions inside the same category, which is the cleanest illustration of why a single adjective like “fresh food prices rose” misrepresents the data. The 2.1% year-on-year fall is the headline comparison and the 7.5% rise is the part households would notice at the fish counter.

Side by side, the living cost index (2.7%) and the fresh food index (-2.1%) tell two stories. The frequently-bought basket rose moderately. The narrow fresh-food basket, dominated by fruit this month, fell. Households experience both at once: stable prices for items they buy every week and meaningful monthly shifts at the fresh food counter. Calling this environment “stable” or “rising” hides the asymmetry, which is the structural point. (A recent article on [stock, housing, and inflation anxiety framing](https://shawnassets.wordpress.com/2026/09/01/what-do-stock-housing-and-inflation-anxiety-claims-measure-in-korea/) makes the same point about not letting three different signals collapse into one composite.)

## 4. The 0.1 percentage point gap between the province and the nation

National September: 120.43, up 0.3% month on month and 2.9% year on year. Gyeonggi September: 120.64, up 0.3% month on month and 3.0% year on year. The 0.1 percentage point gap between the province and the nation is small and not a divergent trend by itself. Both numbers come from the same statistical system on the same day.

The national 2.9% figure is more useful as a benchmark than as a headline. It is 1 percentage point above the Bank of Korea’s 2% objective and roughly midway between the 2022–2023 peak of around 5% and the 2% objective. The September release does not pin a path toward 2%, and this article does not infer one. Whether the next monthly release stays in the 2.9%–3.0% range, drops toward 2.5%, or climbs back to 3.2% is the question the October data will answer directly. ([Earlier coverage of GDP, jobs, and prices](https://shawnassets.wordpress.com/2026/09/06/next-weeks-gdp-jobs-and-prices-how-far-does-koreas-recovery-signal-go/) used the same idea of treating three signals as separate lines, not one composite.)

Treating Gyeonggi’s 3.0% versus the nation’s 2.9% as a policy signal is not supported by a single monthly comparison. A one-month 0.1 percentage point difference is well inside sampling noise for a 458-item index. The more useful reading is “both are in the 3% band, both moved in the same direction last month, neither has reached the 2% objective, and the energy sub-block is the dominant force in both.”

## 5. The reported mention of the “oil ceiling price” policy and why it does not let us conclude a number

In the same paragraph as the eight-category industrial block, the official release added a scenario line: had the government’s “oil ceiling price” program not been in place, the September rise would have traced a “steeper upward curve.” This is a policy-attribution statement, not a quantified counterfactual. The release itself does not publish a number for the counterfactual path, and News1 does not add one. This article therefore does not add one either.

Two readings are valid and we keep them separate. Reading A: the September energy sub-block includes a policy effect, and the energy sub-block would have been higher without it. Reading B: the exact size of that effect, in percentage points of the headline 3.0%, is not in the data and we do not estimate it. A reader who wants to study the size of the effect should look at the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy’s policy page and at the program’s operational definition first, and at separate policy-impact studies second.

We note that the framing matters because “the policy worked” and “the policy reduced X by Y percentage points” are different claims. The release supports the first framing; it does not support the second. The first framing is what we transcribe here, without extending it.

## 6. What September showed and what September did not show

Five direct observations summarize the September release. First, Gyeonggi’s CPI rose 0.3% from the previous month and 3.0% year on year. Second, the eight-category industrial block ran at 4.4%, led by petroleum at 14.7% and durables at 5.2%. Third, agricultural and marine products averaged 0.5%, with double-digit movements inside on pumpkin, mushrooms, mackerel, and hairtail. Fourth, the living cost index ran at 2.7% and the fresh food index fell 2.1% year on year. Fifth, the national September CPI was 2.9% year on year.

Four categories the September release did not isolate are worth naming. First, telecommunications, subscriptions, medical services, and education services are not separately broken out in this release and we do not estimate them. Second, retail shelf prices, promotions, coupons, online-versus-offline gaps, and subscription-delivery averages are not in the index, and we do not extrapolate them. Third, household income and expenditure flows belong to a different statistical series and we do not pair them with the CPI. Fourth, the 31 city-county level movements inside Gyeonggi are not separated in the write-up we cite and we do not infer them.

The third point is the one that confuses readers most often. A 3% CPI rise is not a 3% rise in personal spending, because personal spending depends on the personal basket. Different households in the same province will feel different numbers in any given month. The CPI is an average and this article stays on the average. We do not translate the average into a household-by-household experience.

## 7. What to watch in the next monthly release

The October monthly release will likely arrive in early November. Four questions will determine whether the September picture is durable or a one-month shape.

- Does the Gyeonggi year-on-year rate stay in the 3% band, fall toward 2.5–2.7%, or climb back above 3.2%? The direction answers whether the September energy line was a single-month spike.

- How does the eight-category industrial block’s sub-block mix move? If petroleum stays near 14–15% and durables stays near 5%, we are reading a broad industrial price pressure story. If petroleum eases while durables hold, we are reading an energy story. If both ease, we are reading a broader cooling.

- Do the living cost index and the fresh food index converge or stay split? September’s 2.7% vs -2.1% is a wide gap. A narrower gap means frequently-bought items and fresh items are moving together. A wider gap means the asymmetry is intensifying.

- Does the Gyeonggi-versus-nation spread stay near 0.1 percentage points or widen? A narrow spread keeps the “provincial story is also the national story” reading. A widening spread points to a Gyeonggi-specific driver worth naming.

This article does not issue an October preview. It lays out what to watch because that is what readers can verify with their own eyes when the official release lands. Each of the four signals is something the official release itself will report directly, so readers do not need to take a forecast on faith.

## 8. Limits we deliberately keep

- This article is a September-only read and does not forecast October or November.

- We do not treat the 0.1 percentage point Gyeonggi-versus-nation gap as a policy signal.

- We transcribe the “oil ceiling price” policy mention as scenario language and do not estimate a numerical counterfactual.

- Telecommunications, medical, education, and subscription services are not isolated in the release and we do not estimate them.

- We do not turn “felt inflation” into a single number. The living cost index and the fresh food index are kept as two answers, not one.

- We do not produce buy, sell, rent, borrow, tax, contract, or any other decision recommendation. We do not forecast monetary policy, fiscal policy, or specific corporate outcomes.

These limits are deliberate, not accidental. The September release plus its sources already yields a rich read. Forecasts and recommendations require additional information that this article does not gather, and we leave that work to other venues.
