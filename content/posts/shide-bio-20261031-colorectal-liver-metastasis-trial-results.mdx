Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center  posted  ClinicalTrials.gov results  on  October 30, 2026 , reporting results for  73 people enrolled after colorectal liver metastasis resection , with  one-year relapse-free outcomes .  The systemic chemotherapy differed between groups,  so  the registry cannot isolate bevacizumab’s effect.

 ClinicalTrials.gov first posted this completed trial’s results on October 30, 2026, adding outcome and safety tables to the public record.

The registry update covers group outcomes after hepatic arterial infusion and systemic chemotherapy. It provides estimates and safety counts, but does not identify which treatment component explains them. A careful reading keeps the population, comparison, and missing statistical test in view.

## The reported result

 Design: randomized phase 2, parallel groups

 Enrolled: 73 after resection of colorectal liver metastases

 Primary outcome denominators: 35 and 38

 One-year relapse-free survival

 Relapse-free survival is reported here as each group’s percentage at one year.

The denominators belong to the two assigned groups, not a single pooled set of 73. They also differ from the number who completed treatment. The registry gives each group’s one-year estimate but no hazard ratio or P value testing the difference.

Random assignment placed participants into two groups, but the groups differed in more than bevacizumab: one used oxaliplatin-based systemic chemotherapy and the other irinotecan-based chemotherapy. The registry therefore compares two treatment packages rather than isolating one drug.

The registry’s primary outcome is one-year relapse-free survival, reported as a percentage in each randomized group. It lists 71% (95% CI, 52–83) among 35 participants assigned to bevacizumab with hepatic arterial infusion and systemic chemotherapy, and 83% (95% CI, 66–92) among 38 assigned to infusion and systemic chemotherapy without bevacizumab. These are group estimates, not a reported treatment-effect comparison. The record gives no hazard ratio or P value for this endpoint, so the numerical gap should not be described as proof that either strategy worked better.

The registry reports 73 enrolled participants, with 35 and 38 in the two groups. Its participant-flow table records 24 and 30 as completed and 11 and 8 as not completed; adverse events appear among the listed drop/withdrawal reasons for 6 and 4 participants, respectively. The table does not make those counts a treatment-completion analysis. They also do not establish the analysis population for every outcome. The posted primary-outcome denominators remain the full group counts of 35 and 38.

## What this comparison can tell us

The result applies to the tested packages in people whose colorectal liver metastases were surgically removed. It cannot be carried over to unresected disease or another treatment setting. Overlapping confidence intervals do not prove that the regimens are equivalent.

This result concerns people whose colorectal cancer had spread to the liver and whose liver metastases had been surgically removed. It does not describe treatment for unresected disease, disease in other settings, or people receiving the same drugs for a different reason. The question in this trial was whether adding intravenous bevacizumab to hepatic arterial infusion and systemic chemotherapy could extend the time before relapse after liver surgery.

The posted figures do not answer that question on their own. A percentage at one year is easier to read than a statistical model, but comparing two percentages without a reported between-group estimate leaves out how uncertainty was assessed. The confidence intervals are broad and overlap. That observation does not itself establish that the treatments are equivalent; it means the registry table, as posted, does not supply enough comparative statistics to make a reliable winner claim.

## Safety counts and their limits

Death and adverse-event counts need to stay paired with their group denominators. The registry counts alone do not assign a cause to a death or individual event, so they should not be relabeled as treatment-related mortality or drug-specific event rates.

The registry lists deaths during the study period in both groups: 18 of 35 in the bevacizumab-containing group and 18 of 38 in the group without bevacizumab. These totals are clinically important context, but they are not evidence that treatment caused those deaths. The public record does not provide a causal attribution for each death here. It also does not specify the follow-up duration attached to these death totals; a separate survival outcome is reported at two years. They should not be read as deaths occurring within the one-year relapse-free endpoint window.

The adverse-event summary separately reports serious events in 16 of 35 participants in the bevacizumab-containing group and 10 of 38 in the comparison group. It reports other events in 30 of 35 and 9 of 38. These registry categories are not a direct comparison of treatment-related rates, and they do not identify which events were judged caused by a study drug. The trial was unmasked and the treatment packages differed, so the raw difference between groups cannot be assigned to a single component. The record’s adverse-event tables include event terms such as diarrhea, but their presence does not establish that a particular regimen caused an event in an individual participant.

## What a full report still needs to show

The next useful check is whether the sponsor publishes a full report with the prespecified analysis, the handling of participants who did not complete treatment, and a direct between-group estimate for relapse-free survival. That report would also need to explain how follow-up was handled and whether the registry’s one-year percentages reflect the planned analysis population. Until then, the public table supports a description of what was posted, not a definitive comparison of the regimens.

A full report would also clarify the difference between the trial’s original question and the treatments actually compared. The registry describes bevacizumab as an addition, but its arm descriptions also show different systemic chemotherapy drugs. If the analysis does not separate those components, readers should continue to understand the finding as a comparison of treatment packages. It cannot establish the independent effect of bevacizumab.
