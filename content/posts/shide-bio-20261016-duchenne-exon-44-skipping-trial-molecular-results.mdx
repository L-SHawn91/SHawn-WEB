## The trial measured biological response before clinical benefit

EXPLORE44 tested delpacibart zotadirsen, also called del-zota, an investigational antibody–oligonucleotide conjugate. It is designed to carry an exon-skipping molecule to muscle to restore dystrophin expression for some DMD variants. The goal is a biological response, not proof that a participant will gain strength or walk longer.

**Study design:**  Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, two-part phase 1/2 trial.

**Enrolled population:**  Healthy men aged 18–55 in part A; ambulatory and non-ambulatory men aged 7–27 with DMD44 in part B. Overall, 45 healthy volunteers and 26 participants with DMD44 enrolled.

**Analysis population:**  Safety results covered 34 del-zota and 10 placebo recipients in part A, plus 19 del-zota and 7 placebo recipients in part B; no separate ITT group is named.

**Primary endpoint:**  Incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events.

Part B included nine, ten, and seven participants in the 5 mg/kg, 10 mg/kg, and placebo groups. They received three intravenous doses: 5 mg/kg every 6 weeks or 10 mg/kg every 8 weeks. Exon 44 skipping rose from baseline by 36·6% and 43·4% in the treatment groups; placebo changed 1·6%. The cohorts were assessed at month 4 and month 5, respectively. Because dose and interval both differed, this small study cannot isolate a dose effect. Endpoint-specific denominators are not given, and p values are nominal.

The researchers also reported mean increases from baseline in dystrophin levels of 25·0% and 25·6% in the two del-zota groups, compared with a mean change of -0·7% with placebo. The confidence intervals for the active-treatment estimates were 9.2 to 40.7 and 13.0 to 38.3; the placebo interval was -4.8 to 3.3. These measurements support target engagement, meaning the treatment affected the intended molecular pathway. They do not tell us whether the change was large enough to alter muscle performance or how long it might last.

## The molecular signal answers an early question, not the clinical one

Duchenne muscular dystrophy is caused by changes in the DMD gene that prevent cells from making functional dystrophin. Dystrophin helps protect muscle fibres during contraction. Some variants can be addressed by skipping a selected exon, a step that lets the cell read the remaining genetic message in a different frame. The approach is mutation-specific: these results concern variants amenable to exon 44 skipping, not all people with Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

 Exon skipping is a way of leaving out a targeted section of a genetic message so that the cell may produce a shorter protein.  In this trial, the team measured the share of dystrophin RNA transcripts that skipped exon 44 and the change in dystrophin level. These measures are closer to the drug’s intended action than walking, strength, or independence. They can show a biological response, but cannot substitute for evidence that people feel or function better.

The placebo group helps put the molecular measurements in context, and the reported changes were larger in the active-treatment groups over the short measurement period. Still, the groups were small and the abstract does not establish a dose-related improvement in clinical outcomes. The measured changes are not validated substitutes for patient benefit. Families should not interpret these early results as evidence that the investigational treatment is available or effective for an individual child.

The study included ambulatory and non-ambulatory participants. The public abstract does not give enough subgroup detail to determine whether response differed by age, walking status, or other disease features. Larger studies will need prespecified clinical outcomes and longer follow-up to test changes that matter in everyday life.

## Safety findings include two treatment discontinuations

Safety and tolerability were the trial’s primary objective. In part A, at least one adverse event was reported by 21 of 34 healthy volunteers receiving del-zota and 3 of 10 receiving placebo; the abstract says none were severe or serious. Part B included 19 del-zota recipients across the two cohorts and seven receiving placebo. At least one adverse event was reported by 16 of 19 (84%) on del-zota and 6 of 7 (86%) on placebo; most were mild or moderate.

Two participants in the 5 mg/kg group stopped treatment because of adverse events: one anaphylactic reaction and one infusion-related reaction. The abstract does not provide a Part B serious-adverse-event count or a rate for each dose group. The groups were small, and short follow-up cannot characterize uncommon harms or long-term risks.

The healthy volunteers in part A and participants with DMD44 in part B are different populations, so their safety percentages should not be combined. Part B reports an overall rate for the combined del-zota groups, not separate rates for each dose. The abstract says the trial is complete but gives no death count or full Part B adverse-event table. An event not mentioned in the summary cannot be assumed not to have occurred.

EXPLORE44 is an early phase 1/2 study, not designed to establish slowed disease progression. Target-engagement was assessed at month 4 in the 5 mg/kg cohort and month 5 in the 10 mg/kg cohort; the abstract reports no long-term functional outcomes. The statistical tests were nominal and the groups small. The authors support a phase 3 study to assess efficacy and long-term safety. That recommendation is not proof that clinical benefit has been established.

## A larger trial must show whether target engagement changes daily life

The next study would need to test outcomes that matter directly to participants, alongside continued monitoring for adverse events. It should make clear which participants are included in each analysis, how many contribute to each endpoint, and how results differ across dose groups. Longer observation will be needed to assess durability and safety. These are not minor details: they determine whether a molecular response can be connected to a meaningful clinical change.

The EXPLORE44 report says the findings support further evaluation in a phase 3 study. That is a research direction, not a regulatory decision. The PubMed abstract does not report approval of del-zota. Before a treatment could be considered established, evidence would need to show the balance of benefit and harm in a larger, longer study, with clinical outcomes rather than target engagement alone.

For people living with Duchenne muscular dystrophy, this report adds an early molecular result for one mutation-defined group. It does not answer whether the treatment improves strength, mobility, or quality of life. The evidence has moved one step from laboratory rationale toward clinical testing, but the patient-level question remains open.

This article summarizes the PubMed abstract for the EXPLORE44 phase 1/2 trial. It describes short-term molecular target-engagement and safety findings; it does not establish clinical benefit, long-term safety, or regulatory approval. No endpoint-specific denominators or full Part B serious-adverse-event table were available in the abstract.
