On October 26, 2026, Anthropic filed a Form S-1 registration statement with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. Two numbers sit side by side in that document. First-half 2026 revenue of $16.29 billion, and a net loss over the same six months of $80.75 billion. The loss is roughly five times the revenue. Yet a quarterly table in the same filing contains a different number: $974 million of operating income for the second quarter of 2026, the first black figure on the operating line in the periods this filing covers.

Which of those describes Anthropic? Staring at the headline is not enough. Read in isolation, an $80.7 billion loss looks like a business in trouble. But about 89.6% of that loss is neither an operating loss nor tax. It is the difference that appears when liabilities attached to instruments Anthropic has already issued are valued again.

The numbers in this document sit on two layers, the operating layer and the valuation layer. Without separating the two, the largest number looks like the most important one. The two layers answer different questions, and neither can stand in for the other. Alongside them sits a third change: compute cost fell from 201% of revenue to 55%. The filing does not itself tie that change to the second-quarter operating profit.

## Where the $80.7 Billion Comes From

Start with how the $80.75 billion net loss is built. The S-1 breaks it into four pieces.

The first is the operating loss: $693 million for the first half of 2026, which is $16.286 billion of revenue against $16.979 billion of total operating expenses. At this layer Anthropic is still underwater.

The second is interest income of $1.111 billion for the same half. The filing describes this as interest earned on cash and cash equivalents and short-term investments, which consist primarily of debt securities including US Treasury and agency securities, corporate debt and commercial paper.

The third piece is most of the rest. The change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and SAFEs was a **$72.39 billion loss** for the first half of 2026. The fourth is other income (expense), net, at an $8.76 billion loss. Add those to the operating loss and interest income and you reach a loss before income taxes of $80.73 billion; subtract $21 million of income taxes and the net loss is $80.75 billion.

That is the same mechanism the opening paragraph described, and the footnote locates it precisely. The S-1 footnote attributes the change in fair value primarily to an increase in the fair value of outstanding convertible promissory notes resulting from an increase in the enterprise valuation during each of the respective periods.

The instrument structure makes the mechanism clearer. Convertible notes payable stood at $108.57 billion as of June 30, 2026. The filing ties one conversion rule explicitly to the IPO price: notes issued under the Amazon 2023 NPA and Amazon 2024 NPA convert into Class N common stock at the lower of the fixed conversion price per share in the table and the IPO price on the cover — subject to Amazon’s ownership cap, under which no portion of a note converts if Amazon would then hold more than 12% of the capitalization. Amazon may waive that cap at its own election, and the principal that cannot convert because of it is re-issued as a non-interest-bearing rollover note. The scope matters here. Principal outstanding under those two Amazon NPAs was $6.203 billion as of June 30, 2026, while the $108.57 billion figure is the total convertible notes payable balance the filing reports on the face of the balance sheet. The filing does not tabulate the conversion terms of each component of that larger balance. It does say what drove the change in the first place: an increase in the fair value of the outstanding convertible promissory notes, resulting from an increase in the enterprise valuation, and it adds that because these instruments are marked to market, increases in enterprise valuation drive increases in this expense. The filing does not itself tie that expense to the conversion terms of the full balance, but a higher equity value raising what a convertible note is worth follows from what the notes are.

The argument does not extend to calling the whole $80.7 billion a cash outflow. Nothing in the filing establishes that the $693 million operating loss or the $8.76 billion of other expense are cash items. The only line that can be called non-cash with support is the $72.39 billion fair-value change, and even that is a remeasurement driven by the enterprise valuation increase the footnote cites, not a figure derived from an IPO price.

Second-quarter 2026 revenue of $11.555 billion with $974 million of operating income. That is the first operating profit in the periods this filing covers. Operating margin was 8.4% of revenue.

Two things happen at once here. Revenue ran at 2.44 times the prior quarter’s $4.731 billion, and the same quarter flipped from a $1.667 billion operating loss to $974 million of operating income. Second-quarter revenue is 30.7 times the $376 million recorded in the first quarter of 2025.

Anthropic also reports a company-defined adjusted operating income figure. Adjusted operating income excludes stock-based compensation, non-cash charitable contributions and the Bartz settlement from operating loss, and the filing writes the definition out in full. On that basis second-quarter adjusted operating income was $2.235 billion at a 19% adjusted operating margin, against the 8.4% GAAP operating margin. The filing also gives its reason for the measure: by excluding items management believes are not directly reflective of the ongoing operating performance of the business, it believes these non-GAAP financial measures “provide investors, analysts, and others with meaningful insight and enhance their overall understanding of our business and financial performance by providing a more consistent and comparable overview of our financial performance across periods.” The filing defines the Bartz settlement as the charge recorded in 2025 to settle the Bartz litigation. The two measures answer different questions, and both are printed in the same filing.

Every figure in that quarterly table is unaudited. The S-1 introduces it as “unaudited quarterly consolidated statements of operations data,” states that historical results are not necessarily indicative of the results that may be expected in the future, and adds that the results for any particular quarter are not necessarily indicative of the results to be expected for a full year or any other period. The first black figure is also the most recent quarter in the document, and it carries that same caveat.

## What a 14x Revenue Increase Actually Means

First-half revenue rose from $1,163 million to $16,286 million, which is 14.0 times. The first half of 2026 alone is 3.55 times the full year 2025.

The annual progression is in the document: $41 million in 2023, $386 million in 2024, $4.585 billion in 2025. Revenue grew about 9.4 times in 2024 and about 11.9 times in 2025. Measured per day, $386 million for a full year was roughly $1.06 million, and $4.585 billion was roughly $12.56 million. A single quarter, the second quarter of 2026, comes to about $127 million a day — ten times the 2025 full-year average, and a number the filing reaches without ever printing it.

The filing names the driver itself. Revenue increased sequentially in each quarter presented, and the increases were driven by successive model and product releases that drove higher usage, expansion, and broader adoption, with both consumption-based and subscription-based revenue contributing.

Two structural facts sit alongside it. The first is how revenue is recognized: Anthropic recognizes revenue when customers use the intelligence service, based on the quantity of tokens consumed. Subscriptions are generally prepaid monthly or annually, reset as usage limits are reached, and are therefore recognized on a ratable basis over the subscription period, “which is generally 12 months or less.” The second is the route to market. Claude is accessible on all three major cloud platforms — Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud Platform and Microsoft Azure — and Anthropic operates a joint go-to-market approach with those cloud service providers, establishing direct contractual relationships with customers while paying a fee to each CSP. The filing does not state what share of revenue travels through those three cloud channels.

## The Cost Layer: Compute Fell to 55% of Revenue

The most visible change in the filing is the cost structure. Anthropic’s MD&A includes a table expressing compute and infrastructure expense as a share of revenue.

### H1 2025

**Compute and infrastructure ($M)**
2,334

**Percentage of revenue**
201%

### H1 2026

**Compute and infrastructure ($M)**
8,945

**Percentage of revenue**
55%

The absolute number rose by $6.611 billion, or 283%. Revenue rose 14 times over the same half, so the ratio fell from 201% to 55%. Across the full years, the same figure was 653% in 2024 and 160% in 2025. Among the annual and half-year periods this filing discloses for 2024, 2025 and the first half, compute cost in the first half of 2026 was for the first time smaller than revenue.

The filing does explain the ratio, though not the cash flow. It attributes the increase in compute expense to the delivery of committed compute under the CSP arrangements, and the decline in compute as a percentage of revenue to revenue increasing significantly against a compute base that was primarily committed in advance and did not fluctuate with in-period demand. The 2025 cash flow section says something else: the decrease in operating cash outflow was driven primarily by a decrease in prepaid hosting services reflecting the consumption during 2025 of hosting capacity that had been prepaid in prior periods, and by an increase in accounts payable, accrued expenses and other current liabilities due to increased CSP costs and the timing of related payments. That passage compares 2025 with 2024, and it does not explain the first-half 2026 swing.

Operating cash flow moved in the same direction. Anthropic used $1.513 billion in the first half of 2025 and generated $3.929 billion in the first half of 2026 — a $5.442 billion swing in six months. On an annual basis, operating activities consumed $2.637 billion in 2025, and the first half of 2026 brought the first positive operating cash flow the filing reports.

The other side of that ledger deserves equal attention. Investing activities used $17.181 billion in the first half of 2026 and financing activities brought in $110.774 billion. Cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments stood at $131.651 billion as of June 30, 2026. Anthropic has plenty of cash, but most of that cash arrived through financing activities rather than from operations, and treating $131.7 billion as evidence of balance-sheet strength without that qualification overstates the case.

## The Numbers the Filing Does Not Give You

The S-1 leaves the most consequential IPO numbers blank. This is not editorial trimming. It is the state of the source.

The cover page says the initial public offering price per share “will be between $ and $,” with both figures empty. The aggregate market value row on the cover is empty. Every share count in the offering table is empty: shares of Class A offered by the company, shares offered by the selling stockholders, the underwriters’ option to purchase additional shares, Class A outstanding after the offering, and Class N outstanding after the offering. Class T shows 1 share, Class E shows 0 shares, and Class F shows 1 share.

The Pro Forma and Pro Forma As Adjusted columns of the balance sheet are empty as well; only the Actual column carries figures. The Contractual Obligations table is empty, and one sentence about revenue growth ends mid-figure: revenue “increased from $197 million for the three months ended December 31, 2024 to $ for the three months ended September 30, 2026.”

Those blanks fall into two categories. The first is values that do not exist yet. The offering price, the share counts and the aggregate market value are not disclosed because the IPO price has not been determined. The second is values that are not written down as numbers anywhere in the document. The filing does not say why the Pro Forma columns and the Contractual Obligations table are unpopulated.

One boundary applies here for the reader. **Nothing fills the spaces this document left open.** Carrying an offering price range from press coverage into a filing that does not contain it leads readers to believe the company published it. The most accurate sentence available from this document is that the price has not been set. That is enough.

## How the Equity Is Divided

Anthropic built a complex capital structure before listing. From the actual balance sheet as of June 30, 2026: redeemable convertible preferred stock of $157.510 billion, additional paid-in capital of $5.336 billion, and accumulated deficit of $132.902 billion, producing total stockholders’ deficit of $127.619 billion. The arithmetic is visible: total assets of $147.358 billion, less total liabilities of $117.467 billion and redeemable convertible preferred stock of $157.510 billion, leaves stockholders’ equity at negative $127.619 billion. The preferred sits outside stockholders’ equity, which is what produces the deficit.

There are five classes of stock. Class A carries one vote per share. Class F is held by the Founder LLC, a limited liability company of which the seven co-founders — Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish and Christopher Olah — are initially the sole members, and “will be entitled to an aggregate number of votes that equals 50.1% of the total voting power of our outstanding shares of capital stock with respect to matters submitted to stockholders generally, including the election of our directors other than the Class T directors.” Critically, Class F “will not have any economic rights.” Class T is held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust, which “generally has the authority to elect and remove the Class T directors, who together will constitute a majority of the authorized members of our board of directors,” and it has no economic rights either. Class E is issued to employees excluding the co-founders, votes only on certain limited matters and carries no economic rights. Class N has the same rights as Class A except that it carries no voting rights, and is held primarily by strategic business partners.

What this exposes is not just how the business runs today but who will control it once it is public. The 50.1% in Class F is voting power, not ownership. Class T controls a board majority, not ownership. Class N is held primarily by strategic business partners. And Anthropic states that “We are considered a ‘controlled company’ within the meaning of the Nasdaq Global Select Market corporate governance requirements.” The same section explains why: because the LTBT, as sole holder of the Class T stock, generally has the power to elect a majority of the board’s authorized members, Anthropic may elect not to comply with certain Nasdaq corporate governance requirements, and shareholders “will not have the same protections afforded to stockholders of companies that are subject to all of the Nasdaq Global Select Market corporate governance requirements.” Anthropic says it does not expect to rely on that exemption except with respect to its nominating and corporate governance committee, and notes that its board currently has a majority of independent directors and its audit and compensation committees consist entirely of independent directors. That condition is hard to leave out when judging how the structure will work after listing.

The instrument structure sits on top of that. Note purchase agreement capacity available for issuance totals $77.000 billion: the Google 2026 NPA at $20.000 billion, the Amazon 2026 NPA at $15.000 billion, and the Broadcom Infrastructure Partner 2026 NPA at $42.000 billion. Principal outstanding is $0 in every one of those columns. Separately, Google holds an option to purchase up to $10.0 billion of Class N common stock in a private placement concurrent with the offering at the public offering price, and exercising it would reduce the Google 2026 NPA by a corresponding amount.

Two instrument-level references are tied explicitly to that price. Notes issued under the Amazon 2023 NPA and Amazon 2024 NPA convert against it, and Google’s Class N option is exercised against it. They are not the only such references: net proceeds, RSU settlement tax withholding, the pro forma per-share adjustments and the public-benefit share issuances all start from the offering price too. The public-benefit issuances are the clearest case: the board may issue Class N stock to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund representing up to an additional 2% of then-outstanding shares each time the share price appreciates 50% above the prior price milestone, and that milestone begins with the initial public offering price. The 2% base is defined broadly: it counts shares subject to options, RSUs and other convertible securities, together with shares the Fund already holds. What this article has covered — the operating quarter, the compute ratio, the control structure — is already stated in the filing without reference to a price.

## Are the Customers Growing

The customer figures in the S-1 mix reference dates. The customer-count series runs from December 31, 2023 to September 30, 2026, while the 74% revenue share is measured over the six months ended June 30, 2026.

Customers contributing at least $100,000 of trailing-twelve-month revenue grew from approximately 20 as of December 31, 2023, to approximately 200 as of December 31, 2024, to approximately 1,500 as of December 31, 2025, to more than 11,900 as of September 30, 2026. More than 1,900 customers contributed at least $1 million on a trailing basis and approximately 250 contributed at least $10 million. Approximately 40 million customers contributed less than $100,000.

Customers at $100,000 or more produced approximately 74% of first-half 2026 revenue. That 74% is measured on the six months ended June 30, 2026, while the $100,000 threshold itself is a trailing-twelve-month test. Roughly 40 million customers fall below that line and account for about 26% of revenue, which is 100% minus 74% — our arithmetic, not a number the filing prints, and the filing leaves the trailing-twelve-month share blank. By headcount the two groups are roughly 3,400 to 1, and by revenue the smaller group carries about three quarters.

The remaining headroom is also given as numbers. Over 50% of the Fortune 500 and over 30% of the Global 2000 were customers at $100,000 or more as of September 30, 2026, and the same passage says “our penetration into the largest enterprises remains early” — revenue from those Fortune 500 customers averaged less than 0.1% of their total expenses for the three months ended September 30, 2026. That is as far as the filing goes.

## How Anthropic Writes Its Own Risk

The S-1 risk factors contain this: sufficiently advanced AI systems could, if not developed, deployed and operated with appropriate safeguards, oversight and security measures, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused, replicated, weaponized, or allowed to operate with a high degree of autonomy. And then Anthropic writes in the first person: “We have observed that our models are contributing to the pace of our own research.”

The same section describes agentic systems: agentic AI systems are capable of autonomously taking actions on behalf of users, interacting directly with external systems, executing code, making API calls, sending communications, or completing transactions with limited human oversight. Anthropic placed both sentences in its own risk factors.

That is the part of this document a reader is most likely to skim past. Anthropic, which averaged about $127 million of revenue a day in the second quarter of 2026 and posted an operating profit, writes that its own models are contributing to the pace of its own research, and files the misuse and weaponization of those same systems under its risk factors. A growing company recording the risk attached to that growth in the same filing is as much a part of this document as the operating quarter.

## What to Judge Now, and What Is Still Unknown

Back to the opening question. Revenue of $16.286 billion and a net loss of $80.748 billion came out of the same document, but they describe different layers. 89.6% of that loss is remeasurement, not an operating loss and not tax. And the same document’s quarterly table records the first operating profit in its covered periods: $974 million on $11.555 billion of revenue in the second quarter of 2026. Picking one side and leaving the other out distorts the filing.

The operating layer has changed direction: compute cost fell from 201% of revenue to 55%, and operating cash flow swung $5.442 billion in six months. The valuation layer has no IPO price yet — the offering price and the share counts are both blank, so market capitalization and per-share structure cannot be discussed. Two instrument-level references in the filing are tied explicitly to that price: the conversion price for the notes issued under the Amazon 2023 NPA and Amazon 2024 NPA, and the exercise price of Google’s Class N option. They are not the only ones. The control structure is already described in the document: the filing states that a single share of Class F stock will be issued to the Founder LLC immediately prior to the completion of the offering, and that the Long-Term Benefit Trust generally has the authority to elect and remove the Class T directors, who together constitute a majority of the authorized members of the board.

Three things are unknown. What the IPO price will be, how many shares will be offered, and how the unaudited first-half numbers carry into full-year 2026. Anthropic itself warns that its results for the six months ended June 30, 2026 “are not necessarily indicative of the results that may be expected for the full year ending December 31, 2026,” and this document does not answer it.
