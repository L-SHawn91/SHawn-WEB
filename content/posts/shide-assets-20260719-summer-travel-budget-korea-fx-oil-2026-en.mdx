On the Bank of Korea’s economic statistics system (ECOS), the daily indicator showed the Korean won at 1,499.20 per US dollar as of 05:50 on 11 July 2026. To be clear from the start, that figure is a snapshot of a single moment, not a forecast of where the currency is heading. Still, for anyone planning a summer trip abroad, it is the kind of number that sends you back to the budget spreadsheet.

Here is the short version. Travel costs are not set by the exchange rate alone. Think of a trip as a shopping basket in which only some items carry a foreign-currency price tag. Airfare, fuel surcharges, accommodation, local transport and meals each respond to the exchange rate in different ways, so the accurate move is to break the budget into pieces and recalculate each one separately.

## What a 1,499.20 Reading Actually Tells You

Most exchange-rate figures in the news are quotes or indicators captured at a specific moment. The 1,499.20 reading above means exactly that: it was the value displayed on the ECOS screen at that time. It is not the price you would pay at a bank counter today, because real currency exchange and card payments add each institution’s applied rate and fees on top.

Two habits keep this manageable. First, take your reference rate from an official source such as ECOS rather than from a headline. Second, treat that number as a coordinate for right now, and add the fees you actually pay before doing any arithmetic.

One caution is worth spelling out. Comparing today’s situation against a budget drafted back when the won traded in the 1,300s is like overlaying two maps drawn from different starting points. If you have an older plan, write down the rate and date it assumed, then rebuild the whole budget from today’s figures instead of adjusting the old one by feel.

## Which Parts of a Travel Budget Are FX-Sensitive — and Which Are Not

A trip budget breaks into four blocks: airfare, accommodation, local spending (transport, meals, admissions), and extras such as insurance and roaming. Of these, the block that responds most directly to the exchange rate is local spending paid in foreign currency.

By contrast, anything already priced and paid in won does not move one-for-one with the currency. A ticket listed in won, or a hotel prepaid in won, stays fixed regardless of what the exchange rate does afterward — at least until the seller reprices. Over time, of course, sellers may adjust their won prices to reflect currency and cost changes.

A practical way to sort this: items settled in won before departure — flights, prepaid rooms, tours, insurance — go in a “locked cost” line. Items you will pay in foreign currency on the ground — meals, transport, shopping — go in a “floating cost” line. Even this two-line split shows you immediately how much of your budget actually moves when the exchange rate makes headlines.

The takeaway is simple. When the won weakens, it is the foreign-currency spending you have not yet paid that gets heavier. So the first step in building a budget is sorting items by payment currency, not by size.

## Airfare and Fuel Surcharges: Where Oil Enters the Picture

Airfare is not explained by the exchange rate alone. Seat demand, route competition and oil prices all feed into it. Fuel surcharges in particular are calculated with reference to jet fuel prices, which puts them on a separate axis from the currency.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) noted in a recent commentary that oil briefly neared USD 150 per barrel before easing. The same piece makes another point that matters here: refined products such as gasoline, diesel and jet fuel do not always move in lockstep with crude. Refining margins and regional supply conditions mean the fuel prices consumers actually face can diverge from the crude benchmark.

For a traveler, the practical conclusion is a single rule: do not guess at surcharges with a rough percentage. Check the actual published amount at the time you issue the ticket. Korea’s Air Portal, run by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, publishes aviation information, and the surcharge also appears as a separate line at checkout.

One more structural point: surcharges apply as of the ticketing date, so the same route can carry different surcharges depending on when you buy. In your budget sheet, record “base fare” and “surcharges and taxes” as separate lines — it makes later comparisons much easier.

## Scenario Math: Converting Local Spending Into Won

Local spending follows one formula. Won cost = foreign-currency amount × applied exchange rate × (1 + card or settlement fee rate). Suppose you spend USD 1,000 locally with a 1% fee. The won cost shifts with the rate as follows.

The three rates in the table are working assumptions for practice, not predictions. The point is the structure, not any particular number: on USD 1,000 of spending, every 50-won move in the rate changes your cost by roughly KRW 50,000. Internalizing that sensitivity is the whole purpose of the exercise.

The formula also runs in reverse. If you decide your local-spending ceiling is, say, KRW 1.5 million, divide that ceiling by the rate and the fee factor to back out your foreign-currency limit. If the rate rises above your planning assumption, you shrink the foreign-currency limit — a way of keeping the won total fixed.

Cash and card portions can be split with the same logic. Cash you have already exchanged is locked in at the rate you got; card spending will follow the rate at payment or settlement. Dividing total local spending into “already locked” and “still floating” shows you precisely how much currency exposure remains.

Fee rates differ from person to person, depending on the payment method and the cash-versus-card mix. The accurate input is the fee that actually appears on your own statement. This article does not recommend any particular card or exchange method.

## Accommodation, Transport and Meals: Sort by Payment Currency

Accommodation changes character depending on when and in which currency it is paid. A room prepaid in won is a locked cost. A room paid in foreign currency on arrival is exposed to the exchange rate under the formula above. The same hotel can belong in different columns of your budget depending on how you pay.

A general, widely known caution: when a foreign payment terminal offers to charge you in won instead of the local currency — so-called dynamic currency conversion — an extra conversion margin may apply. Whatever the method, checking the billing currency and fees before confirming a payment keeps budget errors small.

Some transport passes and admission tickets can be purchased in won before departure. The moment such an item is paid, it moves from the floating column to the locked column, shrinking your remaining currency exposure. Prepayment often comes with refund conditions, though, so weigh it against the chance your itinerary changes.

Local transport and meals are the most purely exchange-rate-exposed part of the budget. Rather than padding them vaguely, set a daily foreign-currency allowance and note its won equivalent using the formula — it is the easiest block to manage day by day.

## Comparing Against a Domestic Vacation Without the Optical Illusion

When the currency weakens, it is tempting to jump straight to “let’s just stay domestic.” The instinct is reasonable, but a fair comparison has to be made on totals. Domestic travel prices also move in peak season, and Statistics Korea (KOSTAT) publishes the consumer price data that tracks those categories.

The illusion arises when the two options are measured differently. If the overseas trip is counted as a full total including airfare while the domestic trip is imagined as just a room rate, the conclusion will be distorted in one direction or the other. Build both budgets on the same template — transport, lodging, meals, activities — and set them side by side.

A second illusion is assuming that because the exchange rate rose by some percentage, the total cost of the trip rose by the same percentage. As shown above, only part of the budget is exposed to the currency. An itinerary heavy on won-prepaid items may see total costs rise by less than the rate did; an itinerary heavy on local spending may see the opposite.

Fixing a single comparison date also matters. Pricing the overseas plan at today’s rate while pricing the domestic plan from months-old memory is not a comparison at all. Build both budgets on the same day, from the same reference points.

## A Pre-Departure Checklist of Official Sources

Before locking in a budget, four checks against official sources are worth the time. First, confirm the reference exchange rate on the Bank of Korea’s ECOS. Second, check aviation information, including surcharges, on Korea’s Air Portal. Third, when weighing a domestic alternative, consult Statistics Korea’s price data. Fourth, for background on how oil and fuel prices relate, refer to international sources such as the IEA.

Numbers keep moving. The exchange-rate and oil references in this article are snapshots of official data at the time of writing, so redo the arithmetic with current figures right before you actually pay.
