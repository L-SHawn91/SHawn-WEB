Five-year follow-up published in NEJM Evidence on August 25, 2026 asked a focused clinical question in high-risk locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma: if only the induction regimen changes before the same concurrent chemoradiotherapy, do outcomes change five years later?

The comparison was designed to keep the downstream treatment shared. It asks how TPC versus PF induction relates to failure-free survival and treatment burden in that defined setting, not which regimen is right for every patient.

## Could the first treatment choice shape outcomes five years later?

For people with high-risk locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the choice of induction chemotherapy is made before concurrent chemoradiotherapy begins. This trial tested whether that first choice could change longer-term outcomes.

Its question was narrower than a general comparison of all nasopharyngeal carcinoma care. It enrolled patients with T4N0-2M0 or TanyN3M0 disease, a prespecified high-risk population.

## The induction regimen was the one planned difference

The multicenter randomized trial enrolled 238 patients: 118 were assigned to TPC and 120 to PF. Median follow-up was 89.1 months.

TPC combines paclitaxel, cisplatin, and capecitabine; PF combines cisplatin and fluorouracil. Both groups received two 21-day induction cycles and then moved to the same concurrent chemoradiotherapy phase.

That shared downstream treatment helps isolate the induction choice rather than compare two completely different courses of care. The primary endpoint, failure-free survival, measures time to a protocol-defined failure event such as recurrence or progression.

## The five-year results made the contrast clearer

Five-year failure-free survival was 77.6% with TPC and 62.9% with PF. The hazard ratio was 0.52, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.34 to 0.82.

A hazard ratio compares event rates over follow-up. Here, a value below 1 means fewer failure events were observed in the TPC group; it does not promise the same benefit for an individual patient.

The prespecified secondary endpoints also favored TPC at five years: distant metastasis-free survival was 87.8% versus 78.8%, locoregional relapse-free survival was 92.0% versus 82.1%, and overall survival was 89.6% versus 82.2%. The corresponding hazard ratios were 0.51, 0.43, and 0.51. The public abstract does not report a tumor-response value.

Safety belongs in the same reading of the result. Grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in 68 patients (57.6%) in the TPC group and 79 patients (65.8%) in the PF group, while treatment adherence was similar between groups.

Better failure-free survival does not mean universal cure or universal safety. Adverse-event grades do not show every symptom, its timing, dose modification, laboratory change, or supportive-care decision, so clinical use still requires a patient-specific review of organ function, other medicines, general health, and local treatment capacity.

## What this result changes, and what it still cannot answer

For this exact population and treatment sequence, the trial adds stronger evidence to the discussion of TPC as an induction option. It does not establish that TPC is better than every regimen used in practice, or substitute for guideline recommendations, regulatory approval, and local availability.

The full article and trial record are still needed to assess detailed subgroup effects, missing data, protocol departures, and the pattern of long-term toxicity. How the results apply also depends on stage, Epstein-Barr virus DNA level, general health, planned chemoradiotherapy, and patient preferences.

The practical next step is to ask an oncology team whether the trial eligibility criteria and treatment sequence fit the person in front of them.
