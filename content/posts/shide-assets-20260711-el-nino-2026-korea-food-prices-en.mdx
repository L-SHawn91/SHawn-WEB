Pacific Ocean temperatures can feel remote from the price of cooking oil, bread, eggs, or a restaurant meal in Korea. Yet Korea imports most of its wheat and much of the grain used for animal feed. Weather shocks can therefore travel through harvests, international prices, import costs, and food production before they reach households.

El Niño is already under way and is expected to strengthen. But the latest data do not support calling this a global food-price crisis. The climate risk and the remaining supply buffers need to be read together.

## El Niño has begun and is forecast to strengthen

On 3 July 2026, the World Meteorological Organization said El Niño had developed in the tropical Pacific and was forecast to strengthen rapidly into a strong event during July–September. Seasonal-average sea-surface-temperature anomalies could exceed 2°C in key monitoring regions.

NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center maintained an El Niño Advisory on 9 July. The latest weekly Niño-3.4 index was +1.2°C. NOAA estimated an 81% chance of a very strong El Niño during October–December and a 97% chance that El Niño would persist through early spring 2027.

Those probabilities describe the state and strength of ENSO. They are not probabilities for Korean food inflation. Both WMO and NOAA warn that even strong events do not produce the same outcome in every region.

## Why the official classification matters

“Super El Niño” is widely used in headlines, but WMO explicitly says it is not part of its operational classification. Official products use categories such as weak, moderate, strong, and very strong.

The grounded description today is that El Niño is strengthening rapidly and NOAA sees an 81% chance of a very strong event late this year. That is different from declaring a “super El Niño” officially confirmed.

## Global food prices are not moving in one direction

The FAO Food Price Index averaged 130.3 in June, down 0.3% from May but 2.2% above a year earlier. The cereal index fell 3.5% month on month. International wheat and maize quotations declined 4.4% and 6.2%, respectively.

Vegetable oils moved the other way. The vegetable-oil index rose 3.8% in June and stood 23.3% above its year-earlier level. The all-rice index also increased 3.2% during the month.

Supply buffers remain meaningful. FAO forecasts global cereal output of 2.983 billion tonnes in 2026, 1.9% below the 2025 record but still the second-highest level on record. The global cereal stock-to-use ratio is forecast at 32.0% in 2027. These figures are why the current market cannot simply be described as a food crisis.

## The route from weather to retail prices

El Niño does not affect every region or crop in the same way. Drought in one producing region may coincide with better harvests elsewhere. Crops also have different growing seasons, trade structures, and inventory buffers.

When a weather shock does reach consumers, the path usually looks like this:

- Rainfall and temperature alter crop yields in major producing regions.

- International prices for wheat, maize, soybeans, palm oil, or rice react.

- Exchange rates and shipping costs change Korea’s landed import cost.

- Existing contracts and inventories delay the pass-through.

- Milling, food manufacturing, and feed costs reach retail and restaurant prices only partly and with variable lags.

A strong El Niño alone cannot tell us the size or timing of the final price change. Supply, stocks, currencies, and contracts matter at every step.

## Why Korea is exposed

USDA FAS Seoul’s 2026 Grain and Feed Annual places Korea’s current wheat self-sufficiency rate at roughly 1–2%. Annual compound-feed use is about 21 million tonnes, and corn remains the dominant feed ingredient.

High import dependence creates a wide channel for international prices and exchange rates to influence domestic costs. It does not mean supermarket prices move on the same day or by the same percentage as futures or global benchmarks.

Korea’s June consumer-price details also show divergent conditions. The agricultural cereal category was 8.2% higher than a year earlier, livestock products 6.2%, processed foods 0.9%, and eating out 2.6%. These are current observations, not evidence that El Niño caused the increases.

## What to watch first

Vegetable oils deserve attention because the FAO index was 23.3% above a year earlier in June. Palm and soybean oils are used widely in processed foods and food service.

Wheat quotations declined in June, but FAO forecasts global wheat output to fall 4.3% from last year. The next question is whether crop outlooks in major exporters deteriorate further.

Maize and soybean meal reach households indirectly through feed costs and livestock products. That route is slower and also depends on animal inventories, disease, and domestic production conditions. One international price series cannot predict egg or meat prices by itself.

Three monthly checkpoints are enough for a disciplined read:

- NOAA’s ENSO strength and persistence probabilities

- FAO’s food, cereal, and vegetable-oil indices plus production and stock forecasts

- Korea’s CPI details for cereals, livestock products, processed foods, and eating out

## Follow the path, not the panic

The current evidence points in two directions at once. El Niño is strengthening and weather risks have increased. At the same time, global cereal output and stocks still provide buffers, and cereal prices fell month on month in June.

The useful task is not to declare a crisis. It is to watch whether weather changes become actual crop losses, whether international prices and exchange rates move together, and how those costs pass through Korea’s importing, processing, retail, and food-service layers.
