## The registry separates enrollment from treatment exposure

The first number to keep in view is the denominator behind the response result. The registry counted 102 enrolled patients, 95 for whom treatment was attempted, and 91 who received study drug treatment. The 36.3% ORR therefore belongs to the treated cohort reported in the result table, not automatically to every person who entered screening or enrollment. The record describes melphalan/HDS delivered through percutaneous hepatic perfusion, a liver-directed procedure intended for people whose metastatic uveal melanoma was predominantly in the liver.

**Design:** Phase 3, multicenter, single-arm, open-label treatment study.

**Enrolled:** 102 patients.

**Treated cohort:** 91 patients received study drug.

**Primary endpoint:** objective response rate by independent central review.

That structure matters because the trial was not designed to compare melphalan/HDS with another treatment. It was multicenter and used independent central review of imaging, but it remained open-label and single-arm. The registry result can tell readers how the treated cohort performed against the prespecified response framework. It cannot, by itself, tell us how much of that outcome came from the treatment rather than patient selection, prior care, disease distribution, or the natural course of a difficult cancer. The enrollment count is still useful: it shows the scale of the record and why the three population counts should not be collapsed into one headline number.

## The response signal comes with a wide clinical question

The registry reports an ORR of 36.3%, with a 95% confidence interval from 26.44% to 47.01%.  Objective response rate is the proportion of participants whose tumors met the protocol’s complete or partial response criteria on imaging.  It is not the same as cure, longer survival, or a guarantee that a particular patient will respond. The same record reports a median duration of response of 14.00 months, median overall survival of 20.53 months, and median progression-free survival of 9.03 months. It also lists a disease control rate of 73.6%.

Those numbers answer different questions. Response rate describes the share meeting an imaging threshold. Duration of response asks how long a confirmed response lasted. Overall survival and progression-free survival include time-to-event definitions that depend on the analysis population and follow-up. Reading them as one combined promise would make the registry say more than it does. The confidence interval around the response estimate also leaves room for uncertainty, especially because there was no randomized comparator.

The linked PubMed abstract gives the same broad clinical context and describes the FOCUS study as an open-label, single-arm, multicenter phase 3 study. That independent publication is useful for interpretation, but the news trigger here is the later registry result posting. A registry update and a peer-reviewed article should remain separate evidence layers even when they describe the same trial.

## The safety table needs its own denominator and time window

The most important caution is easy to miss if the response percentage is read first. In the ClinicalTrials.gov adverse-event table, the Melphalan/HDS group lists 67 deaths among 91 at-risk participants. The table covers serious and other adverse events from first dose until 30 days after the last dose, with assessment reported up to June 2021 and approximately 61 months. This is an all-cause table count within the registry’s safety reporting structure. It is not a statement that melphalan/HDS caused those deaths, and it should not be compared directly with the 102-person enrollment count.

The same table lists serious adverse events in 43 of 95 at-risk participants and other adverse events in 95 of 95. The linked PubMed abstract separately states that no treatment-related deaths were observed. These statements are not interchangeable: an all-cause death count, a serious-event denominator, and a treatment-attribution statement answer different questions. The registry also lists thrombocytopenia in 9 of 95 and neutropenia in 8 of 95 as serious events, while the abstract names thrombocytopenia and neutropenia among the most common serious treatment-emergent adverse events.

For readers, the practical boundary is simple: the registry shows what was recorded, while causality requires a fuller clinical review. The record is not a randomized safety comparison, and the safety population is not identical to the efficacy population.

## The next judgment depends on comparison and population detail

The registry update makes the trial easier to inspect, but it does not close the clinical question. A useful next step is to place the response and survival estimates beside an appropriate comparator or a clearly defined historical benchmark, while keeping the eligibility criteria and prior treatment history visible. That comparison would need to account for the fact that 102 people enrolled, 95 had treatment attempted, and 91 received study drug. Mixing those groups would change the denominator and could make the result look stronger or weaker than the record supports.

Readers should also look for the complete analysis definitions, missing-data handling, response confirmation, duration of follow-up, and adverse-event attribution. The 2026 results posting changes access to the registry evidence; it does not change the study design, create regulatory approval, or turn a single-arm result into proof of comparative benefit. The most defensible takeaway is narrower: the official record now makes a detailed cohort result available, and its usefulness depends on reading efficacy, safety, and uncertainty together.
