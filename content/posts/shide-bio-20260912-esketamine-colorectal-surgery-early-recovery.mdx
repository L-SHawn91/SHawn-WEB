The Xiangya Hospital research team , reporting in  the Journal of Investigative Surgery   on September 11, 2026 , studied  adults undergoing elective laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer  and reported  lower early postoperative HADS-A and HADS-D scores  after intravenous esketamine was added to anesthesia and analgesia.  The PubMed abstract does not report the enrolled sample size or the exact size of the score differences , while  the finding is limited to early postoperative mental-health scores rather than cancer control or survival .

 The article entered the public record on September 11, 2026 , when PubMed recorded its online publication, making the early postoperative result timely to examine without extending it to long-term cancer outcomes.

## A postoperative question, tested during colorectal cancer surgery

The question behind the report is narrower than the headline of a new cancer treatment. The researchers asked whether adding intravenous esketamine to anesthesia and patient-controlled analgesia was associated with less anxiety and depression soon after laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer. That is a question about the experience of recovery, not about whether the operation removed a tumor or changed a patient’s cancer outlook.

**Design**: prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled study with 1:1 assignment.

**Participants**: adults having elective laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer; the abstract does not state the enrolled number.

**Analysis**: group-level postoperative comparisons are reported, but exact efficacy and safety denominators are not given in the abstract.

**Primary outcome**: Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale score on postoperative day 1.

The cohort was adult surgical patients, and the intervention was a defined esketamine regimen layered onto anesthesia and postoperative analgesia. Because the PubMed abstract leaves the enrollment and analysis denominators unstated, the result can be described as a direction of between-group difference, not as a percentage or an effect size reconstructed from the article summary.

Random assignment and a control group give the comparison a clearer structure than a before-and-after observation. They still do not make every postoperative difference attributable to esketamine, especially when the public abstract does not show the participant flow, baseline scores, or numerical size of the changes.

The abstract reports lower HADS-A and HADS-D scores in the esketamine group on postoperative days 1 and 3. It also reports a lower 24 h interleukin-6 level and higher patient satisfaction compared with the control group. The listed secondary outcomes included sleep quality, level of consciousness, complications, hospital stay, inflammatory factors, and satisfaction, but the abstract does not provide the numerical results for each of those outcomes.

## The result concerns the first days after an operation

 HADS is a questionnaire that separates anxiety and depression symptoms during medical care.  A lower score on that questionnaire can tell clinicians that people reported fewer symptoms at a particular time. It is not the same as a psychiatric diagnosis, and it does not by itself show that a medicine prevents depression or anxiety after discharge.

The timing matters here. The primary outcome was set for postoperative day 1, with further HADS assessments on postoperative days 3 and 7. The reported difference on days 1 and 3 therefore speaks to the early recovery period described in the abstract. It does not tell us whether the groups remained different at later follow-up, whether symptoms affected daily life after discharge, or whether the result persisted through cancer treatment.

The dosing details also explain what was compared. The esketamine group received 0.5 mg/kg during induction and had 1 mg/kg added to patient-controlled intravenous analgesia. That is a study regimen, not a general instruction for patients or a dose recommendation. The control group received conventional general anesthesia and patient-controlled analgesia, so the comparison was between care pathways rather than an isolated tablet-versus-placebo experiment.

For a patient facing surgery, early anxiety and low mood are meaningful experiences to measure. The report suggests that the esketamine pathway was associated with better scores during the first part of recovery, but the missing sample size and effect estimates make it impossible to judge how large or durable that difference was from the abstract alone.

## What the abstract leaves open about safety and scale

The clearest limitation is numerical. The PubMed abstract identifies the population and the comparison but does not state how many people enrolled, how many reached each assessment, or how missing scores were handled. Without those denominators, a reader should not convert the report into a response rate, calculate an unreported treatment effect, or assume that the groups were large enough to detect uncommon complications.

The abstract names postoperative consciousness, complication rate, hospital stay, inflammatory factors, and satisfaction as outcomes. It reports a lower interleukin-6 level at 24 h and higher satisfaction, but it does not give the corresponding values, confidence intervals, or a complete adverse-event breakdown in the record used here. That means the available text cannot support a claim that the regimen was broadly safer, shortened hospitalization, or improved every part of recovery.

The design does provide a comparator and a prospective randomized structure, which are useful safeguards against some forms of confounding. They do not remove the need to inspect the full publication for participant flow, baseline balance, protocol deviations, concurrent analgesics, and the way the many secondary outcomes were handled. Those details affect how much weight to place on a result that appears across several early time points.

Nothing in the public abstract establishes a benefit for colorectal cancer control, recurrence, or survival. It also does not establish long-term psychiatric benefit. The safest reading is limited to the reported early postoperative scores and the listed short-term comparisons; medication decisions belong with the treating clinical team, which can consider the full paper and the individual patient’s risks.

## The next read should add the numbers before the claim grows

The full article is needed to answer the questions the abstract cannot. Readers should look for the enrollment flow, the number included in the primary analysis, the baseline HADS values, the between-group difference with uncertainty, and the results at postoperative days 3 and 7. The same check applies to complications and sedation or consciousness outcomes: a direction without a denominator cannot show how often an event occurred.

It is also worth separating the clinical question from the treatment setting. Esketamine was used around a defined operation with a defined analgesia plan. A result in that setting cannot automatically be transferred to another operation, a person who was not in the trial population, or a different dosing schedule. The report’s higher satisfaction finding may be relevant to recovery research, but it is not a promise that every patient will feel better.

The answer to the original question is therefore modest. In adults undergoing elective laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer, the PubMed abstract reports lower early HADS-A and HADS-D scores with the esketamine pathway than with control care. The record supports a reason to read the full trial, not a conclusion about cancer treatment, long-term mental health, or a personal medication choice.

As the complete data become available, the useful comparison will be the size, precision, and durability of the difference alongside complications and patient flow. Until then, the early postoperative signal should stay attached to the narrow population and time window in which it was reported.

 **Evidence boundary**
This article uses the official PubMed record for the randomized, placebo-controlled postoperative study.
