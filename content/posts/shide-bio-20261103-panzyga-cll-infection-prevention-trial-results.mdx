Octapharma , reporting on  ClinicalTrials.gov , posted Phase III results on  2026-11-02  for  247 people with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and hypogammaglobulinemia , with  14 of 124 Panzyga recipients and 27 of 123 placebo recipients recorded as having a major infection .  The registry gives no statistical comparison for that difference , so  the counts are a result to examine, not proof that Panzyga prevented infections .

The results first appeared in the public registry on  November 2, 2026, the first results-posting date shown in the record .

## A lower count appears in the Panzyga arm, but the registry stops short of a comparison

**Design:**  Randomized, quadruple-masked Phase III prevention trial

**Enrolled:**  247 people with chronic lymphocytic leukemia and hypogammaglobulinemia

**Randomized groups:**  124 assigned to Panzyga and 123 to placebo

**Primary endpoint:**  Number of participants with a registry-defined major infection

The registry records major infections in 14 of the 124 participants assigned to Panzyga and 27 of the 123 assigned to placebo. Those denominators are the randomized group sizes, not the number who completed follow-up. The cited outcome table does not provide a confidence interval, a p value, or a formal between-group test for this endpoint. The result therefore describes what the registry reports; by itself, it cannot tell readers how likely the observed difference is to reflect a treatment effect rather than chance or other features of the trial.

Random assignment means participants were placed into groups by chance. A placebo comparison can help separate the added treatment from standard care, but it does not replace the statistical analysis needed to judge the size and uncertainty of a difference.

The primary outcome was not every cold or infection mentioned in a medical record. The registry definition covered bacterial or viral infections resulting in death; documented infections requiring anti-infective treatment, with several specified infections counted as major only when hospitalization was needed or extended; and fever of unknown origin that led to hospitalization or a longer stay.

The comparison was between Panzyga added to usual infection prophylaxis and a saline placebo added to the same standard care. It was not Panzyga versus no prevention. The registered regimen was an infusion every four weeks, and the placebo group also received standard prophylaxis.

The registry also reports an overall infection rate of 1.15 per person-year in the Panzyga arm and 1.65 in the placebo arm. The rate counts all infections relative to time observed, while the primary endpoint counts participants who experienced a narrowly defined major infection. These measures are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## The trial tested prevention on top of standard care in a specific CLL population

The trial enrolled people with CLL and  hypogammaglobulinemia, a low level of immunoglobulins . Its clinical question was whether adding Panzyga to standard infection prevention changed the occurrence of major infections in this group. That does not make the result a general test of immunoglobulin treatment for every person with CLL, or for people with other blood cancers or immune conditions.

The study was randomized and placebo-controlled, and the registry describes masking of participants, care providers, investigators, and outcome assessors. These design choices can reduce the influence of expectations on treatment and on how outcomes are judged. They do not repair missing statistical results, eliminate differences in follow-up, or make a registry table equivalent to a full peer-reviewed report.

Recruitment took place at 57 centers in 10 countries. The record says 336 people were screened and 247 entered the randomized groups.

For readers, the main point is modest but concrete. The posted counts move in one direction, and the person-year infection rates do too. Yet the cited major-infection outcome table does not show the uncertainty around that count difference. Without that, it is premature to convert “fewer observed infections” into a claim of established prevention benefit.

The results were posted to ClinicalTrials.gov, where sponsors submit structured information about a study. Such records can make results available before a journal paper appears, but they are not a substitute for the full methods, analysis plan, and peer review. Here, the overall status is listed as completed. That says the study is no longer active; it does not mean that the intervention was proven effective or approved for this use.

## Safety tables include deaths and events regardless of treatment relationship

The registry’s adverse-event module lists 9 deaths among 124 participants in the Panzyga group and 7 among 123 in the placebo group. Those are deaths recorded in the trial groups, not a statement that Panzyga caused them. The registry table alone does not establish the cause of each death or whether an event was related to treatment, so readers should not interpret these counts as evidence of drug-caused harm or as proof of equal risk.

The same module lists serious adverse events in 34 of 124 Panzyga participants and 43 of 123 placebo participants. It also lists adverse events in 70 of 124 participants in the Panzyga group and 69 of 123 in the placebo group. These are participant counts, not event counts, and the overall “any event” measure should not be confused with events considered serious.

Among the other events listed, neutropenia was recorded in 15 of 124 Panzyga participants and 23 of 123 placebo participants. COVID-19 appeared in 17 of 124 and 19 of 123, while upper respiratory tract infections appeared in 12 of 124 and 21 of 123, respectively. The registry describes event collection across the treatment regimen and includes events of any relationship during treatment or up to 30 days afterward. It does not break these events down by time period. The figures therefore cannot be read as treatment-attributed rates.

Mortality follow-up was listed as three years, while the adverse-event module gives a 52-week time frame for the event tables. A full report would be needed to understand causes, timing, and the investigators’ assessment of relatedness.

Participation also did not always continue to the end. The record says 94 of 124 Panzyga-assigned participants and 89 of 123 placebo-assigned participants completed the study. It lists adverse events as the reason for discontinuation for 16 and 10 participants, respectively. Those details belong beside the outcome counts: losing participants before completion can affect what is observed, and a registry summary does not by itself show the effect of missing follow-up on the result.

## The public record needs a statistical comparison and fuller safety context

The next useful evidence is not another headline built around the raw counts. Readers need the prespecified analysis, an effect estimate with uncertainty, and a clear account of how participants who stopped early were handled. The record currently displays group totals for the major-infection endpoint without those statistical details.

A complete publication should also explain which infections met the trial definition, how outcomes were reviewed, and whether the analyses followed the original protocol. For safety, it should clarify the recorded deaths and serious events, including timing and whether investigators considered them related to treatment. That information would help distinguish the registry’s raw reporting categories from a clinical assessment.

The observed numbers are worth noticing, but they cannot carry more weight than the record supports. At present, ClinicalTrials.gov reports fewer participants with a major infection in the Panzyga arm, alongside deaths and adverse events in both groups. The entry does not establish statistical significance, causation, or a treatment recommendation.

For someone considering care, the registry is not a basis for starting or stopping immunoglobulin treatment. Decisions depend on an individual’s diagnosis, infection history, other treatments, and clinician assessment. A clinician can explain whether evidence from this specific population applies to a particular patient.
