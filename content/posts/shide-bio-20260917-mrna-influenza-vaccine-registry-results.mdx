## The registry result favors mRNA-1010 on its prespecified influenza endpoint

The results table concerns adults aged 50 years and older who were randomized to one injection of mRNA-1010 or a licensed inactivated seasonal influenza vaccine. For the efficacy comparison, the registry used a per-protocol set of n=20179 in the mRNA-1010 group and n=20124 in the comparator group. The table records 411 first protocol-defined, RT-PCR-confirmed influenza-like illness cases in the mRNA-1010 group and 577 in the licensed-vaccine group during follow-up that lasted through the end of influenza season, up to 7.5 months.

The trial was randomized, parallel-group, active-controlled, and triple-masked for participants, investigators, and outcome assessors. That setup compares two vaccine strategies under the same trial conditions; it does not compare vaccination with no vaccination.

**Design**: Phase 3 randomized, observer-blind, active-controlled trial.

**Enrolled**: 40,805 adults randomized; mRNA-1010 n=20402 and comparator n=20403.

**Efficacy set**: Per-protocol n=20179 and n=20124.

**Endpoint**: First RT-PCR-confirmed, protocol-defined influenza-like illness from Day 14 through season end.

The registry reports relative vaccine efficacy of 26.6% with a 95% confidence interval from 16.7 to 35.4 and a p value below 0.0001.  Relative vaccine efficacy is the registry’s percentage reduction in illness hazard for mRNA-1010 versus the licensed vaccine.  Those values support the registry’s superiority classification for its prespecified endpoint, but they do not turn the product into a proven choice for every influenza outcome or every age group.

## The comparison is about tested influenza-like illness, not every infection

The endpoint is narrower than the phrase flu prevention can sound in a headline. Participants needed a systemic symptom, such as feverishness, chills, tiredness, headache, or myalgia, plus a respiratory symptom such as sore throat, cough, sputum, wheezing, or difficulty breathing. A positive RT-PCR result then had to fit the protocol definition. The registry therefore gives a clear answer to one prespecified question: how often did the two vaccine groups experience a first qualifying illness episode after the initial post-vaccination window?

Because both groups received active vaccination, the 411-versus-577 comparison speaks to relative performance against the licensed comparator. It does not give a placebo-controlled estimate, and it does not establish protection against hospitalization, severe disease, transmission, or strains outside the recorded endpoint. The per-protocol analysis also excluded important protocol deviations that could affect vaccine response, which can make the comparison cleaner while narrowing the population to whom the estimate applies.

The numerical result is most useful when its denominator stays visible. A case count without the analysis set can make two unlike populations look interchangeable. Here, the registry identifies the denominators and the follow-up window, so the reader can see what the reported relative efficacy does and does not cover.

## The safety record changes how the efficacy result should be read

The registry’s safety window runs from Day 1 through Day 181, and its all-cause mortality table is based on all randomized participants rather than only the efficacy set. It lists 40 deaths among mRNA-1010 participants with n=20402 at risk and 34 deaths among comparator participants with n=20403 at risk. These are all-cause counts in the registry record, not a finding that vaccination caused those deaths. The table must not be rewritten as a treatment-related mortality comparison.

Solicited local and systemic reactions were collected in a smaller safety set through Day 7. The record lists 2283 participants with a solicited reaction among n=3015 mRNA-1010 recipients, compared with 1400 among n=2997 licensed-vaccine recipients. For the broader safety set through Day 181, the registry lists 455 participants with serious adverse events among n=20350 mRNA-1010 recipients and 392 among n=20353 comparator recipients. A serious adverse event is a regulatory category, not a synonym for a vaccine-caused event; causality requires the relevant clinical review.

A second limitation is documentary rather than biological. The linked journal abstract and the registry table do not show the same comparator case total. This post keeps 577 because it is the value in the results table first posted to the registry on September 16, 2026, and it does not treat the two records as interchangeable. The reason for the difference is not established by the materials used here.

That boundary matters more than a neat winner-versus-loser story. The efficacy result is positive for the recorded endpoint, while the reactogenicity and safety tables describe trade-offs and events that need their own interpretation.

## The next check is whether the registry and paper tell the same story

The next reading should put the registry table beside the full journal report and ask which analysis population produced each number. The endpoint wording, event definitions, missing data, strain-specific results, and the attribution of adverse events all deserve a direct comparison. A summary that moves between enrolled participants, safety participants, and per-protocol participants without naming the denominator can sound precise while quietly changing the question.

For now, the dated registry result supports a bounded statement: in this phase 3 comparison of adults aged 50 years and older, mRNA-1010 had fewer recorded first protocol-defined RT-PCR-confirmed influenza-like illness cases than the licensed comparator in the posted table. It does not answer whether the same difference appears in another season, another age group, or routine clinical practice. Those questions require the complete evidence record and should not be filled in from this one posting.
